## 1. Purpose

This module fixes the words that modules m1 and m3 use: walk, length of a
walk, trail, path, cycle, distance, and "a path between n_i and n_j". It
serves question 3 of the brief (the definition of a path between n_i and
n_j) directly, and questions 1 and 2 indirectly: m1 counts walks, m3 needs
"a walk can be cut down to a path" and "a path has at most N - 1 edges".

It establishes:

1. The textbook's Definitions 1.2.10 to 1.2.16 with their numbers, and one
   table of the three naming conventions (this paper = course, textbook,
   English).
2. The definition of a path between n_i and n_j under the course
   convention (no repeated vertex).
3. Lemma 2.2: every walk from u to v contains a path from u to v that is
   no longer than the walk (also for u = v).
4. Corollary 2.3: a path in a graph with N vertices has length at most
   N - 1.
5. Proposition 2.4: a walk, a trail and a path between two vertices exist
   together; so the textbook's Definition 2.1.1 of connected (which says
   "path" in its trail sense) and the board's agree.
6. Proposition 2.5: the textbook's distance equals the least walk length
   and the least path length.

## 2. Prerequisites

All defined here; none is owned by another module.

- Graph G = (V, E): a finite set V of vertices and a set E of edges, each
  edge a two-element subset of V (textbook Definition 1.1.1, p. 9; simple
  means no loops and no repeated edges). The board's vertex names are
  n_1, ..., n_N, so V = {n_1, ..., n_N}, N = |V|; the textbook's name
  for a vertex is v_i. The board's e_{ij} is the edge joining n_i and
  n_j; the textbook writes uv for the edge joining u and v (C11), and the
  board's "e_{ij} in V" is the textbook's "uv in E". In these notes u, v,
  w also stand for vertices.
- Adjacent (textbook Definition 1.1.4, p. 11, C12): u and v are adjacent
  when uv is in E. In a simple graph a vertex is not adjacent to itself.
- The adjacency matrix A and its powers are owned by m1; this module uses
  A only in the cross-check of Section 5.8 and in Remark 2.7.

## 3. Explanation from first principles

Each definition is followed by a remark (what it allows and forbids), an
example and a non-example, as the study-note register asks. "In plain

### 3.1 Convention on length 0 (this paper's own)

A single vertex u, written (u), counts as a walk of length 0 from u to u.
The textbook's Definitions 1.2.10 to 1.2.16 do not say this in words; its
usage assumes it: property 1 of distance on p. 23 (d(u,v) = 0 exactly when
u = v) and the reflexivity of its relation on p. 49 (C26, `m2-11`,
interpretation). Grinberg Definition 2.9.1(a) (p. 36) also has k >= 0
(C47). So the paper states length 0 as its own convention, and the sources
agree with it.

### 3.2 The definitions, in the textbook's order

Notation (as the textbook opens its section): let G = (V, E) be a finite
simple graph and u_0, ..., u_k in V.

**Definition 1 (walk; textbook Definition 1.2.10, p. 21, C17).** A walk of
length k >= 0 from u_0 to u_k, also a (u_0, u_k)-walk, is a sequence
u_0, e_1, u_1, ..., e_k, u_k in which e_i is the edge u_{i-1}u_i for
1 <= i <= k. In a simple graph the edges are fixed by the vertices, so
the walk is written by its vertices u_0, u_1, ..., u_k, each next vertex
adjacent to the one before.

In plain words: you stand on a dot and walk along lines, one line after
another, and you may go back or loop around as often as you like. The
length is how many lines you walked, counting a line again each time you
use it.

Remark (p. 22, C18): vertices and edges may repeat; the length counts each
edge as often as it occurs.
Example: on the board graph (edges n1n2, n2n3), n1, n2, n1, n2, n3 is a
walk of length 4.
Non-example: n1, n3 is not a walk of the board graph, since n1n3 is not an
edge. In the textbook's Fig. 1.2.5, v3, v1, v4, v2, v3, v5 is not a walk
(`m2-9`).

**Definition 2 (closed walk; textbook Definition 1.2.11, p. 22, C19).** A
(u_0, u_k)-walk is closed if u_0 = u_k.

In plain words: a walk that ends where it began.

Remark: length 0 walks are closed; so is n1, n2, n1, which goes along one
edge and back.
Example: n2, n1, n2, n3, n2 on the board graph.
Non-example: n1, n2, n3.

**Definition 3 (trail; textbook Definition 1.2.12, p. 22, C20, where the
textbook calls it "path", ճանապարհ).** A (u_0, u_k)-walk is a trail if the
edges u_0u_1, ..., u_{k-1}u_k are pairwise distinct. Its length is the
number of its edges.

In plain words: a walk that never uses the same line twice. It may visit a
dot again.

Remark: vertices may repeat, edges may not. Grinberg Definition 3.4.5
(p. 101) and Exercise 2.16(d) (p. 51) use "trail" with this meaning
(`m2-6`).
Example (textbook, Fig. 1.2.5): v1, v3, v2, v4, v3, v5 is a trail.
Non-example (textbook): v1, v3, v2, v4, v3, v2, v4, v3, v5, which uses
v2v4 twice.

**Definition 4 (path; textbook Definition 1.2.13, "simple path", պարզ
ճանապարհ, p. 22, C21).** A (u_0, u_k)-walk is a path (course word; the
textbook's simple path) if its vertices u_0, ..., u_k are pairwise
distinct.

In plain words: a walk that never visits the same dot twice.

Remark: the textbook defines the simple path as a trail with distinct
vertices; Lemma 2.1 shows that a walk with distinct vertices is already a
trail, so "a walk with no repeated vertex" is the same thing. Every path
is a trail, and every trail is a walk, but not conversely.
Example (textbook): v1, v3, v4, v5.
Non-example (textbook): v1, v3, v2, v4, v3, v5 (a trail, but v3 twice).

**Definition 5 (path between n_i and n_j; the answer to question 3).** Let
n_i, n_j be in V. A path between n_i and n_j (from n_i to n_j) is a
sequence n_i = u_0, u_1, ..., u_k = n_j with k >= 0 such that u_{t-1} and
u_t are adjacent for 1 <= t <= k and u_0, ..., u_k are pairwise distinct.
Its length is k. This is Definition 4 with u_0 = n_i and u_k = n_j.

In plain words: a way to get from one dot to another along lines without
visiting any dot twice.

Remark: for i different from j such a path has at least one edge; for
i = j the only path is the single vertex (n_i) of length 0, because
u_0 = u_k with k >= 1 would repeat a vertex. There may be several paths
between two vertices, of different lengths. That the course reads "path"
this way rests on the orchestrator's summary of the user's statements,
not on the user's words (`m2-12`, qualified); the textbook definition
itself is supported.
Example: on the board graph the only path from n1 to n3 is n1, n2, n3
(script, Section 5.8, check 1).
Non-examples: n1, n2, n1, n2, n3 (a walk that repeats n1); n1, n3 (not a
walk); n1, n2, n1 as a "path from n1 to n1" (the only path from n1 to n1
is (n1)).

**Definition 6 (closed trail and cycle; textbook Definition 1.2.14, p. 22,
C22).** The textbook calls a closed trail a "closed path or cycle" (փակ
ճանապարհ կամ ցիկլ).

**Definition 7 (simple cycle; textbook Definition 1.2.15, p. 22, C23).** A
cycle in the sense of Definition 6 is simple if only its first and last
vertices repeat. This is the English cycle. The textbook writes C_n for the
simple cycle on n vertices, with n >= 3, and P_n for the simple path on n
vertices (p. 22).

In plain words: a trip that starts and ends on the same dot and visits no
other dot twice. It needs at least three lines.

Remark: Lemma 2.6 shows that in a simple graph a simple cycle has length
at least 3, and that it equals Grinberg's cycle (Definition 2.10.1(b),
p. 47, `m2-7`). The textbook's "closed path or cycle" is wider: two
triangles sharing a vertex give a closed trail of length 6 that is not a
simple cycle (Section 5.7, `m2-13`).
Example (textbook): v1, v3, v4, v2, v1; and n1, n2, n3, n1 if the board
drawing is read literally as a triangle (the matrix says otherwise; m1
reports the mismatch).
Non-example (textbook): v3, v2, v1, v3, v5, v4, v3 is a closed trail but
repeats v3 in the middle. The board graph, whose matrix has edges n1n2 and
n2n3 only, has no cycle at all, and n1, n2, n1 is a closed walk that is
not even a trail.
Length 0 convention: this paper requires positive length for a cycle; the
textbook's C_n has n >= 3, so it agrees (own convention, open question 3).

**Definition 8 (distance; textbook Definition 1.2.16, pp. 22 to 23,
C24).** The distance d(u,v) is the length of a shortest (u,v)-path in the
textbook's sense (a shortest trail) if one exists, and infinity otherwise.

In plain words: the fewest lines you must walk to get from one dot to
another, or "never" if no walk exists.

Remark: Proposition 2.5 shows that "shortest trail", "shortest walk" and
"shortest path" give the same number, so the paper may say "shortest
path" (course sense) without changing the textbook's value; without that
proposition the words would silently change meaning.
Example: on the board graph d(n1,n3) = 2 and d(n1,n1) = 0.
Non-example of a finite distance: add an isolated vertex n4 to the board
graph; then d(n1,n4) is infinity (script, check 1).

## 5. Derivations, worked examples, reproducible calculations

All statements are about a finite simple graph G = (V, E) with |V| = N. A
statement is "derived" when the proof is printed here; the textbook or
Grinberg states some of them, as noted in each first sentence.

### 5.1 Lemma 2.1 (distinct vertices give distinct edges)

Statement (derived; also implicit in the textbook's Definition 1.2.13,
which asks for both). A walk u_0, ..., u_k whose vertices are pairwise
distinct has pairwise distinct edges.

Proof. Suppose the edges e_i = {u_{i-1}, u_i} and e_j = {u_{j-1}, u_j}
are equal for some 1 <= i < j <= k. The two-element sets are equal, so
either u_{i-1} = u_{j-1} or u_{i-1} = u_j. The first forces i - 1 = j - 1
because the vertices are distinct, contradicting i < j. The second forces
i - 1 = j, contradicting i < j. So no two edges are equal. QED

Consequence: Definition 4 (no repeated vertex) and the textbook's
Definition 1.2.13 (a trail with no repeated vertex) describe the same
walks (`m2-1`).

### 5.2 Lemma 2.2 (every walk contains a path)

Statement (derived; the textbook's Lemma 1.2.1, p. 23, covers u different
from v and gives no length bound; Grinberg Corollary 2.9.7, p. 39, has the
bound and covers u = v). Let W = u_0, ..., u_k be a walk from u to v. Then
there is a path from u to v of length at most k, whose edges are edges of
W.

Proof, by strong induction on k.
Base, k = 0: W = (u), whose only vertex is not repeated; W is itself a
path of length 0.
Step: assume the statement for all walks shorter than k, k >= 1, and let
W have length k. If the vertices of W are pairwise distinct, W is a path
of length k, done. Otherwise there are indices i < j with u_i = u_j. Form
W' = u_0, ..., u_i, u_{j+1}, ..., u_k (if j = k it is u_0, ..., u_i). It is
a walk, because the only new consecutive pair is u_i, u_{j+1}, and u_i =
u_j is adjacent to u_{j+1}. It starts at u, ends at v, and has length
k - (j - i) < k, since j - i >= 1. Its edges are edges of W. By the
induction hypothesis W' contains a path from u to v of length at most
k - (j - i) <= k. QED

The textbook's proof is the same cut, with a base case at k = 1 because
it assumes u different from v; allowing k = 0 covers u = v at no cost.
Script check 3 shows the cut on the textbook's walk of length 8: it turns
v1, v3, v2, v4, v3, v2, v4, v3, v5 into v1, v3, v5. Script section 5 applies
the procedure to every walk of length 0..8 on all 8 labelled simple graphs
on 3 vertices, 0..7 on all 64 graphs on 4 vertices, and 0..5 on all 1024
graphs on 5 vertices (2052, 72664, 743680 walks): zero failures. This is
a check, not part of the proof.

### 5.3 Corollary 2.3 (length bound)

A path of length k has k + 1 pairwise distinct vertices among N, so
k + 1 <= N, that is k <= N - 1 (Grinberg p. 40, C49, `m2-3`). With Lemma
2.2: if some walk, of any length, joins u to v, then some path of length at
most N - 1 joins them. The bound is attained: the path graph on N vertices
has a path of length N - 1 (script: longest path seen is 2, 3, 4 for
N = 3, 4, 5). This is the step m3 needs to restrict the test to A^1, ...,
A^(N-1).

### 5.4 Proposition 2.4 (walk, trail, path exist together)

Statement (derived). For vertices u and v of G the following are
equivalent: (a) a (u,v)-walk exists; (b) a (u,v)-trail exists; (c) a
(u,v)-path exists.

Proof. (c) implies (b): a path is a trail by Lemma 2.1. (b) implies (a): a
trail is a walk. (a) implies (c): Lemma 2.2. QED

Hence the textbook's Definition 2.1.1 (which asks for a trail, in its
word "path"), the board's definition ("there exists a path between nodes"
read with the course path) and a definition by walks select the same
graphs (`m2-4`). Grinberg Proposition 2.9.10 (C50) states (a) iff (c) but
not the trail case, so the trail case is proved here.

### 5.5 Proposition 2.5 (distance)

Statement (derived; the textbook states properties 1 to 3 on p. 23 without
proof, `m2-10`). Let L_w(u,v), L_t(u,v), L_p(u,v) be the least length of a
walk, trail, path from u to v (infinity if none). Then L_w = L_t = L_p =
d(u,v), where d is the textbook's distance.

Proof. Every path is a trail and every trail a walk (Lemma 2.1), so
L_w <= L_t <= L_p. Let W be a walk of least length L_w, if one exists. If
W repeated a vertex, the cut of Lemma 2.2 would give a shorter walk,
contradicting minimality; so W is a path and L_p <= L_w. Hence all three
agree. The textbook's d is L_t by Definition 1.2.16. If no walk exists all
three are infinity. QED

Properties (proved from walks): (1) d(u,v) >= 0 and d(u,v) = 0 exactly
when u = v, because a walk of length 0 from u ends at u (needs the length 0
convention); (2) d(u,v) = d(v,u), because reversing a walk gives a walk of
the same length; (3) d(u,v) <= d(u,w) + d(w,v), because a walk from u to w
followed by a walk from w to v is a walk from u to v whose length is the
sum (trivially true if either distance is infinity).

Script check: for every ordered pair in every labelled simple graph on 3,
4 and 5 vertices, breadth-first search level, least trail length (by
exhaustive trail search), least walk length and least path length
agree: zero disagreements.

Remark 2.7 (pointer to m1 and m3, not proved here). By m1's theorem
(A^k)_{ij} > 0 exactly when a walk of length k from n_i to n_j exists;
with Proposition 2.5, d(n_i, n_j) is the least k with (A^k)_{ij} > 0, with
A^0 = I. Paramadevan and Sotheeswaran Theorem 3 (C46) states this for
connected graphs. m1 or m3 owns the statement.

### 5.6 Lemma 2.6 (the simple cycle)

Statement (derived). In a simple graph, a closed walk w_0, ..., w_k of
positive length is a simple cycle in the textbook's sense (a closed trail
whose only repeated vertices are the first and last) iff k >= 3 and
w_0, ..., w_{k-1} are pairwise distinct, which is Grinberg's cycle.

Proof. (textbook implies Grinberg) Only first and last vertices repeat, so
w_0, ..., w_{k-1} are distinct. k = 1 would need a loop w_0w_0, not in a
simple graph. k = 2 gives w_0, w_1, w_0, which uses the edge w_0w_1 twice
and is not a trail. So k >= 3. (Grinberg implies textbook) Only w_k = w_0
repeats. Suppose two edges are equal, {w_{i-1}, w_i} = {w_{j-1}, w_j}
for 1 <= i < j <= k, indices read modulo k. Distinct vertices have distinct
positions modulo k, so either i - 1 = j - 1 (impossible, since 0 < j - i
< k) or i - 1 = j and i = j - 1 modulo k, which gives j - i = 1 and j - i =
-1 modulo k, hence 2 = 0 modulo k, so k <= 2, contradicting k >= 3. So the
walk is a closed trail. QED

This also explains the textbook's C_n with n >= 3 (`m2-8`).

### 5.7 Closed trails that are not simple cycles

In the graph on n1, ..., n5 with edges n1n2, n2n3, n1n3, n1n4, n4n5, n1n5,
the sequence n1, n2, n3, n1, n4, n5, n1 is a closed trail of length 6 (its
six edges are distinct) and not a simple cycle, since n1 repeats in the
middle (`m2-13`, script check 4).

## 6. Assumptions and their reasons

1. Finite simple undirected graph: the brief's assumption (stated by the
   user, per the summary, C9 qualified).
2. Walks of length 0 exist (Section 3.1): needed for d(u,u) = 0, for one
   path from a vertex to itself, and for Lemma 2.2 with u = v; the
   textbook's usage agrees. Without it, A^0 = I would not count walks.
3. "Path" means no repeated vertex (`m2-12`, qualified): the user's course
   convention per the summary; textbook Definition 1.2.13 gives the exact
   wording.

## 7. Limitations, failure cases, competing explanations

- Conventions compete. The textbook's "path" is the English trail; Bai
  and Grinberg call the vertex-distinct walk a path (`C20`, `C21`, `C39`,
  `C47`). A reader mixing sources will misread "path". Paramadevan and
  Sotheeswaran never define the word (base evidence); that is m1's issue.
- The textbook's Lemma 1.2.1 has no length bound and excludes u = v; the
  paper's Lemma 2.2 is the stronger statement. Do not attribute the bound
  or the u = v case to the textbook.
- The textbook's distance is defined through trails; the equivalence with
  "shortest path" is proved in Proposition 2.5, not in the book.
- The textbook's Definition 2.1.1 uses trails (see Proposition 2.4). A
  paper that copies it while using "path" in the course sense must cite
  Proposition 2.4.
- The textbook's "closed path or cycle" is not a cycle in the English
  sense (Section 5.7). Use "cycle" only for Definition 7.
- Figure facts. The Fig. 1.2.5 edges are read from the image; the book's
  own examples settle every statement made, but the figure's v1v5 edge
  and the v2v3 diagonal are thin lines easy to miss. The paper should say
  "the examples use seven edges", not that the figure has seven.
- The script's exhaustive checks cover at most 5 vertices. They check
  Lemma 2.1, 2.2 and Proposition 2.5 for those sizes only; the proofs in
  Sections 5.1 to 5.5 do not depend on size.
- The English column of the table rests on two sources (`m2-14`).
