## 3. Explanation from first principles

**In plain words (suggested, to be fitted to the story the author chooses in the introduction; towns and roads is Grinberg's own picture, p. 36).** Think of towns joined by roads. A walk is a trip that follows roads, one after another. You may pass the same town, or the same road, again and again. The length of a trip is the number of roads you drove, counting each road every time you drive it. Stricter kinds of trip forbid repeats. The strictest, where you never visit a town twice, is what our course calls a path.

### Definition 1.2.10 (walk, closed walk is 1.2.11) [textbook, C23, C24]

A *walk of length k* from u_0 to u_k, or a (u_0, u_k)-walk, is a sequence u_0, e_1, u_1, ..., u_(k-1), e_k, u_k of vertices and edges of G with e_i = u_(i-1)u_i for 1 <= i <= k. It is abbreviated by its vertex sequence u_0, u_1, ..., u_k.

- Remark (the book's, C24): vertices and edges may repeat; the length counts each edge as often as it occurs in the walk.
- Convention of the paper: k = 0 is allowed, a single vertex is a walk of length 0 from u_0 to u_0. The book does not say (the proof of its Lemma 1.2.1 starts at k = 1); Grinberg allows k >= 0 (Definition 2.9.1(a)). This is part of C-m2-06.
- Remark on vertex sequences (derived, C-m2-05): the book's graphs have no loops and at most one edge between two vertices (book p. 9, graph as an antireflexive symmetric relation). So in G the only candidate for e_i is the one edge u_(i-1)u_i, and a sequence of vertices is a walk exactly when consecutive vertices are adjacent. A walk is therefore determined by its vertex sequence. This is why m1 can count walks as vertex sequences, and why the book's walk is Grinberg's walk (Definition 2.9.1(a), C32).
- Example (Fig. 1.2.5 of the book, graph G, printed p. 21): v_1, v_3, v_2, v_4, v_3, v_2, v_4, v_3, v_5 is a walk of length 8 (C-m2-08). Non-example: v_3, v_1, v_4, v_2, v_3, v_5 is not a walk, because v_1v_4 is not an edge (the book says the second of its two sequences is not a walk). The edge list I read from the figure is v_1v_2, v_1v_3, v_1v_5, v_2v_3, v_2v_4, v_3v_4, v_3v_5, v_4v_5 (C-m2-09, qualified: my reading of a picture; the script confirms that every book example is classified as the book says).
- Board example: for the board matrix (edges n_1n_2 and n_2n_3), n_1, n_2, n_1 is a walk of length 2; n_1, n_3 is not a walk.

### Definition 1.2.11 (closed walk) [textbook, C25]

A (u_0, u_k)-walk is *closed* if u_0 = u_k.

- Remark: forbids nothing else; a closed walk may repeat anything. A walk of length 0 is closed.
- Example: n_1, n_2, n_1 on the board graph. Non-example: n_1, n_2, n_3.

### Definition 1.2.12 (the book's "path", English trail) [textbook, C26]

A (u_0, u_k)-walk is a *path* of the book, or (u_0, u_k)-path, if the edges u_0u_1, ..., u_(k-1)u_k are pairwise distinct. Its length |P| is the number of its edges. In this paper I call this object a *trail* when I need to separate it from the course path, and I say "the book's path" when quoting.

- Remark: vertices may repeat, edges may not.
- Example (book): v_1, v_3, v_2, v_4, v_3, v_5 is a trail. Non-example (book): the length-8 walk above, which uses v_3v_2 and v_2v_4 twice.

### Definition 1.2.13 (the book's "simple path", the course "path") [textbook, C27]

A (u_0, u_k)-path of the book is a *simple (u_0, u_k)-path* if all its vertices are pairwise distinct.

- Remark: this is the course convention (C36, below). A simple path is always a trail (Lemma 2), so Definition 1.2.13 only adds a restriction.
- Example (book): v_1, v_3, v_4, v_5. Non-example (book): v_1, v_3, v_2, v_4, v_3, v_5 is a trail but not a simple path, since v_3 occurs twice.

### Definition 1.2.14 (closed path or cycle) and 1.2.15 (simple cycle) [textbook, C28, C29]

A (u_0, u_k)-path of the book is a *closed path* or *cycle* if it is a closed walk, that is u_0 = u_k. A cycle is *simple* if only its first and last vertices repeat.

- Remark: the book's cycle is a closed trail, so it may pass a vertex twice; the simple cycle is the English cycle (convention table below, qualified).
- Remark (derived, C-m2-06): in a simple graph a closed trail of length 1 would be a loop and one of length 2, u, w, u, would use the edge uw twice. So a cycle of positive length has length at least 3. The script finds none of length 1 or 2 in all graphs with at most 5 vertices.
- Example (book): v_1, v_3, v_4, v_2, v_1 is a simple cycle. Non-example (book): v_3, v_2, v_1, v_3, v_5, v_4, v_3 is a cycle but not simple (v_3 occurs three times).
- Board graph (derived): it has no cycle of positive length. A trail uses distinct edges, so a trail here has length at most 2 (there are two edges), and a closed trail of positive length needs length at least 3 (C-m2-06).

### Definition 1.2.16 (distance) [textbook, C30]

d_G(u, v) is the length of a shortest (u, v)-path of the book if one exists, and +infinity otherwise. The book lists three properties: d(u, v) >= 0 with equality exactly when u = v; d(u, v) = d(v, u); d(u, w) <= d(u, v) + d(v, w). The reading of this definition is settled by Proposition 4 below.

### Definition M2.1 (path between n_i and n_j, the course convention) [convention, C36, C-m2-10]

**Course convention.** The board defines "connected" with the word path and does not define path. The user states that the course means a walk with no repeated vertex. So: a *path between n_i and n_j* is a sequence n_i = w_0, w_1, ..., w_k = n_j of pairwise distinct vertices with w_(t-1) adjacent to w_t for 1 <= t <= k. This is the book's simple path of Definition 1.2.13. The paper must present it as the user's stated convention, not as something found in the board or in a file (C36 is `qualified` for this reason).

- Remark: for i different from j the length is k >= 1. For i = j the only path is the single vertex n_i, of length 0, because a sequence of length at least 1 from n_i to n_i repeats n_i (C-m2-10). This is the exact reason a walk such as n_1, n_2, n_1 counted in (A^2)_11 is not a path (m1's counterexample).
- Example: on the board graph, n_1, n_2, n_3 is a path between n_1 and n_3 of length 2. Non-example: n_1, n_2, n_1, n_2, n_3 is a walk between n_1 and n_3 but not a path.

### Lemma 1 (every walk contains a path, with a length bound) [derived; textbook Lemma 1.2.1 weaker; Grinberg Corollary 2.9.7]

**Statement.** Let u and v be vertices of G (equal or not). If there is a (u, v)-walk of length k, then there is a (u, v)-path of length at most k whose vertices are pairwise distinct.

The textbook's Lemma 1.2.1 (C31) assumes u different from v and gives no length bound; Grinberg's Corollary 2.9.7 (C33) has both. I prove the strong form so the paper does not depend on a source for the bound.

**In plain words.** If your trip visits a town twice, you went around a loop. Cut the loop out and you still get from start to end. Repeat until no town repeats. The trip only got shorter.

**Proof.** Strong induction on k, the length of the walk.
- Base, k = 0. The walk is the single vertex u, so u = v, and that walk has distinct vertices (there is one), so it is a path of length 0 <= 0.
- Step. Let k >= 1 and assume the statement for all walks of length less than k, between any two vertices. Let w_0, ..., w_k be a (u, v)-walk. If w_0, ..., w_k are pairwise distinct, it is already a path of length k. Otherwise choose indices i < j with w_i = w_j. Delete w_(i+1), ..., w_j to get w_0, ..., w_i, w_(j+1), ..., w_k. Consecutive vertices are still adjacent: inside the first part and the last part nothing changed, and at the join, w_i = w_j is adjacent to w_(j+1) (if j = k there is no join and the sequence ends at w_i = w_k = v). The new sequence starts at u and ends at v, and its length is k - (j - i) < k, because j - i >= 1. By the induction hypothesis it contains a (u, v)-path of length at most k - (j - i), hence at most k. Done. (This is the book's argument for Lemma 1.2.1, with u = v and the bound added.) End of proof.

Check: the script cuts all walks of length at most 6 in all 1099 labelled simple graphs on at most 5 vertices (2,102,873 walks) and finds 0 violations. Single worked reduction from the output: v_1, v_3, v_2, v_4, v_3, v_2, v_4, v_3, v_5 becomes v_1, v_3, v_5.

### Lemma 2 (a path of the course is a trail) [derived, C-m2-02]

**Statement.** If the vertices of a walk are pairwise distinct, then its edges are pairwise distinct.

**In plain words.** If you never visit a town twice, you cannot drive the same road twice.

**Proof.** Let w_0, ..., w_k have distinct vertices and suppose e_i = e_j with 1 <= i < j <= k, that is {w_(i-1), w_i} = {w_(j-1), w_j}. Then w_(i-1) is w_(j-1) or w_j. The indices j - 1 and j both differ from i - 1, since j - 1 >= i. Two different positions hold the same vertex, contradicting distinctness. End of proof.

### Proposition 3 (the three readings of "joined" agree; connected under Definition 2.1.1 equals the simple-path reading) [derived, C-m2-03; answers C37]

**Statement.** For vertices u and v of G these are equivalent: (a) there is a (u, v)-walk; (b) there is a (u, v)-trail, the book's path of Definition 1.2.12; (c) there is a (u, v)-path with distinct vertices, the course path.

**In plain words.** It makes no difference whether "you can get there" is checked with any trip, with a trip that never repeats a road, or with a trip that never repeats a town. All three answers are always the same.

**Proof.** (c) implies (b) by Lemma 2. (b) implies (a) because a trail is a walk by Definition 1.2.12. (a) implies (c) by Lemma 1, applied to a walk of any length. End of proof.

**Consequence.** Definition 2.1.1 (C39) says G is connected if every two vertices u, v have a (u, v)-path of the book, that is, by Definition 1.2.12, a trail. By Proposition 3(b) iff (c), this holds exactly when every two vertices are joined by a course path. The board's definition (C6) with the course convention (C36) and the book's Definition 2.1.1 therefore define the same graphs. This resolves the contradiction recorded between C39 and C36 and supplies the proof that C37 requested. Grinberg's Proposition 2.9.10 (C34) is the (a) iff (c) part only and says nothing about trails.

### Proposition 4 (the length bound and the form m3 needs) [derived, C-m2-04, C35]

**Statement.** (i) Every path in G has length at most N - 1. (ii) If u and v are different vertices joined by a walk, they are joined by a walk of length k with 1 <= k <= N - 1.

**In plain words.** A trip that never visits a town twice can visit each town at most once, so it uses at most one road fewer than there are towns.

**Proof.** (i) A path of length k has k + 1 pairwise distinct vertices, all in V, so k + 1 <= N. (ii) Apply Lemma 1 to the given walk: a path of length at most that of the walk exists. Its length is at least 1 because u differs from v, and at most N - 1 by (i). A path is a walk. End of proof.

### Proposition 5 (distance is the least walk length) [derived, C-m2-04]

**Statement.** If a (u, v)-walk exists, then d_G(u, v) of Definition 1.2.16 equals the least length of a (u, v)-walk, the least length of a (u, v)-trail, and the least length of a course (u, v)-path, and it is at most N - 1. If no walk exists, all three sets are empty and d = +infinity.

**Proof.** Let a = least walk length, b = least trail length, c = least path length; a <= b <= c because every path is a trail (Lemma 2) and every trail is a walk. By Lemma 1 a shortest walk contains a path no longer than it, so c <= a. So a = b = c, and d = b by Definition 1.2.16. The bound comes from Proposition 4(i). If a walk exists, a trail exists (Proposition 3), so emptiness is the same for all three. End of proof.

**The three book properties** follow from this reading. (1) Lengths are >= 0, and a walk of length 0 exists only from a vertex to itself, which exists for every vertex; so d(u, v) = 0 exactly when u = v. (2) Reversing a walk gives a walk of the same length from v to u, so d(u, v) = d(v, u). (3) A shortest (u, v)-walk followed by a shortest (v, w)-walk is a (u, w)-walk of length d(u, v) + d(v, w), so d(u, w) <= d(u, v) + d(v, w); if either right-hand term is +infinity the inequality holds trivially. The book states these properties without proof (C30).

**Use for m1 and m3.** d(n_i, n_j) is the least k with (A^k)_ij > 0, once m1 shows that (A^k)_ij counts walks (C16).

## 6. Assumptions and their reasons

- Finite simple undirected graph, as the brief states. Why: the book's graphs (p. 9) are of this kind, and the proofs above use no loops and at most one edge per vertex pair.
- k = 0 is allowed for walks. Why: it makes Lemma 1 true for u = v and gives d(u, u) = 0; Grinberg does the same; the book is silent.
- "Path" in the course means no repeated vertex (Definition 1.2.13). Why: the user states it; the board does not define path (C6, C36).
- The English names in the table are taken from Wikipedia for trail and from Grinberg for walk and path. Why: no other source read names a trail; this is disclosed as tertiary.
- Printed page numbers of the textbook. Why: `graph-sources.md`.

## 7. Limitations, failure cases, competing explanations

- Multigraphs and loops: with parallel edges a vertex sequence no longer determines the walk, the remark on vertex sequences fails, and a closed trail of length 2 exists (two parallel edges). Out of scope; the paper says so in one sentence. Directed graphs: reversal in Proposition 5 and the symmetry property fail. Not checked here beyond this remark (unverified).
- The textbook's Lemma 1.2.1 is stated for u different from v. A reader who quotes it for u = v, or for a length bound, is using more than the book states.
- Where sources disagree on "path" (book: trail; Grinberg and the course: no repeated vertex; Bai: closed allowed) the reader can mistake results. The paper must say which meaning is in force at each statement. The word "path" for the book's trail should not appear without "the book's".
- The English column for closed trail and cycle is not backed by a read source (table note). A competing reading of "cycle" in English texts (simple closed path) is the usual one but not sourced here.
- Part 3 of the script bounds walk length by 6 and vertices by 5; it is a test, not a proof.
- Access limit: Wikipedia was read through the fetch tool's summary, so its quotes are as returned, not confirmed against the raw page (source `wikipedia2026path`, access partial).
