## 1. Purpose

This module fixes the words that the other two modules use: walk, trail, path, cycle, length, distance. It serves question 3 (what is a path between n_i and n_j) directly. It serves questions 1 and 2 indirectly: m1 counts walks, and m3 needs the lemma that a walk can be shortened to a path. It establishes:

- the textbook's Definitions 1.2.10 to 1.2.16, with its numbers, in its order;
- one table of the three naming conventions (course and textbook, English, and the paper's choice);
- the definition of a path between n_i and n_j under the course convention;
- the lemma that every walk from u to v contains a path from u to v, with a length bound and with u = v allowed;
- that "there is a walk", "there is a trail" and "there is a path" between two vertices are the same statement, so the three readings of "connected" agree;
- distance, and the bound d(u,v) <= N - 1 when it is finite.

## 2. Prerequisites

Nothing from m1 or m3. Defined here before use:

- **Graph.** G = (V, E): V a finite set of vertices, E a set of edges, each edge a two-element subset {u, v} of V, written uv (textbook Definition 1.1.1, p. 9). "Simple" means no loops (no edge uu) and no two edges with the same two ends. "Undirected" means uv and vu are the same edge. Board notation: the board writes (G, V) for the graph and e_{ij} in V for "edge"; this is the textbook's G = (V, E) and e_{ij} in E (brief, section 7). The paper names vertices n_1, ..., n_N; the textbook's v_i is the same thing.
- **Adjacent.** u and v are adjacent if uv is an edge (Definition 1.1.4, p. 11).
- **Sequence of vertices u_0, u_1, ..., u_k:** an ordered list; repeats are allowed unless stated.
- **N:** the number of vertices, N = |V|.

The adjacency matrix A and its powers belong to m1; connected and component belong to m3 (this module only supplies the words they use).

## 3. Explanation from first principles

**Established facts (textbook), with the paper's comments.** Throughout, G = (V, E) is a simple graph and u, v are vertices.

1. **Walk, Definition 1.2.10 (p. 21).** A walk of length k from u_0 to u_k is a sequence u_0, e_1, u_1, ..., u_{k-1}, e_k, u_k of vertices and edges with e_i = u_{i-1}u_i for 1 <= i <= k. The book then writes the walk as the vertex sequence u_0, ..., u_k, assuming each vertex is adjacent to the one before it (C10). Remark on p. 22: vertices and edges may repeat, and the length counts every edge each time it occurs.
   - Fact (derived, M2-1): in a simple graph the edge between two adjacent vertices is unique, so a walk is the same as its vertex sequence. A walk of length k is k + 1 vertices, each adjacent to the previous one.
   - Convention (interpretation, M2-2, qualified): the paper allows k = 0, a single vertex. The book does not say either way, and its Lemma 1.2.1 excludes u = v. The reason for the choice: (A^0) is the identity matrix in m1, and "every walk contains a path" is cleaner when u = v is included.
2. **Closed walk, Definition 1.2.11 (p. 22).** A walk with u_0 = u_k (C11).
3. **"Path", Definition 1.2.12 (p. 22).** A walk whose edges e_1, ..., e_k are pairwise distinct. This is called a path (Armenian: chanaparh) in the book. In English it is a **trail** (C12, M2-3). Its length |P| is the number of edges.
4. **Simple path, Definition 1.2.13 (p. 22).** A (u_0, u_k)-path of 1.2.12 whose vertices are pairwise distinct (C13). In English this is simply a **path**.
5. **Cycle, Definition 1.2.14 (p. 22).** A closed trail (the book's "closed path or cycle"): u_0 = u_k and the edges are distinct (C14, M2-9).
6. **Simple cycle, Definition 1.2.15 (p. 22).** A cycle in which only the first and last vertices repeat. The book writes C_n for the simple cycle on n vertices, n >= 3, and P_n for the simple path on n vertices.
7. **Distance, Definition 1.2.16 (pp. 22 to 23).** d(u,v) is the length of a shortest (u,v)-path if one exists, and +infinity otherwise (C15). The book lists three properties: d >= 0 with d(u,v) = 0 exactly when u = v; d(u,v) = d(v,u); d(u,v) <= d(u,w) + d(w,v).
8. **Lemma 1.2.1 (p. 23).** For distinct u and v, every (u,v)-walk contains a simple (u,v)-path; proof by induction on the length (C16).

**Why the naming is a trap, and how the paper handles it.** The book uses the word "path" for Definition 1.2.12 and needs the extra word "simple" for the strict version. English texts (and the lecture, by the user's statement) use "path" for the strict version. Both usages are legitimate; mixing them is the danger. Table 1 is the paper's single reference. The paper writes **path** for "no repeated vertex" everywhere and writes **trail** for the book's "path"; whenever it cites Definition 1.2.12 it says so.

Table 1. The three conventions (claims C12, C13, M2-3).

| What it forbids | Book's term | Number | English term | Paper's term |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| nothing (vertices and edges may repeat) | walk (shrjanc'um) | 1.2.10 | walk | walk |
| a repeated edge | path (chanaparh) | 1.2.12 | trail | trail |
| a repeated vertex | simple path (parz chanaparh) | 1.2.13 | path | path |
| closed, no repeated edge | cycle (cikl) | 1.2.14 | closed trail | cycle (as the book) |
| closed, only the ends repeat | simple cycle | 1.2.15 | cycle | simple cycle |

Warning kept explicit: in English "cycle" usually means the book's "simple cycle". The paper writes "simple cycle" when it means that.

**The definition of a path between n_i and n_j (question 3).** Under the course convention (interpretation M2-11, qualified: the user's statement, recorded in the brief, plus the book's 1.2.13):

> A path between n_i and n_j is a sequence of vertices w_0, w_1, ..., w_k with w_0 = n_i, w_k = n_j, with w_{t-1} and w_t adjacent for 1 <= t <= k, and with w_0, ..., w_k pairwise distinct. The number k is its length.

For i different from j this is textbook Definition 1.2.13. For i = j the only such sequence is (n_i), of length 0 (under the convention in M2-2). The board's "path between nodes" in the definition of connected is read this way; Proposition 2.4 below shows the reading does not change which graphs are connected.

**In plain words (for the paper's layer).** A walk is a trip where you may revisit places and reuse roads. A trail is a trip where no road is used twice. A path is a trip where no place is visited twice. Every path is a trail and every trail is a walk, but not the other way round.

## 5. Derivations and worked examples

Notation as in the textbook: G = (V, E) simple, u, v, w in V.

**Proposition 2.1 (a walk is its vertex sequence).** In a simple graph, for adjacent a and b the edge ab is the only edge with ends a and b. Hence two walks with the same vertex sequence are the same walk, and the walks of length k from u to v correspond one to one to the sequences u = x_0, ..., x_k = v with x_{t-1}, x_t adjacent. (M2-1.) Proof: Definition 1.2.10 forces e_t = u_{t-1}u_t; the set {u_{t-1}, u_t} determines the edge since there are no multiple edges. This is why m1 can count vertex sequences.

**Proposition 2.2 (path, trail, walk).** Every path is a trail, and every trail is a walk; neither converse holds. (M2-4.)
Proof. A trail is a walk by Definition 1.2.12. Let u_0, ..., u_k be a path (all vertices distinct) and suppose the edges e_i = u_{i-1}u_i and e_j = u_{j-1}u_j are equal with i < j. Then u_{i-1} is one of the two ends u_{j-1}, u_j of e_j. Both have an index larger than i - 1, so u_{i-1} equals a different vertex of the sequence, contradicting distinctness. So the edges are distinct. Strictness: on the textbook's graph of Fig. 1.2.5 (below), v1, v3, v2, v4, v3, v5 is a trail whose vertex v3 repeats, so a trail that is not a path; v1, v3, v2, v4, v3, v2, v4, v3, v5 reuses the edges v2v3, v2v4 and v3v4, so it is a walk that is not a trail. The textbook gives the same two examples. QED.

**The example graph.** The textbook's Fig. 1.2.5 (p. 21), graph G, has five vertices. Reading the page image I take its edges to be v1v2, v1v3, v1v5, v2v3, v2v4, v3v4, v3v5, v4v5 (M2-5; the book does not print an edge list, and v1v5 is the edge none of the book's examples uses). Script part 1 confirms the book's statements on this graph:

- v1,v3,v2,v4,v3,v2,v4,v3,v5 is a walk of length 8 (the book: "eight").
- v3,v1,v4,v2,v3,v5 is not a walk, since v1v4 is not an edge.
- v1,v3,v2,v4,v3,v5: a trail, not a path. v1,v3,v4,v5: a path.
- v3,v2,v1,v3,v5,v4,v3: a closed trail (a cycle in the book's sense), not simple. v1,v3,v4,v2,v1: a simple cycle.

**Lemma 2.3 (a walk contains a path).** Let G be a simple graph, and let u_0, ..., u_k be a walk from u to v (u = v allowed, k >= 0). Then there is a path from u to v of length at most k, whose vertices are some of u_0, ..., u_k in the same order. (M2-6; textbook Lemma 1.2.1 for u different from v, Grinberg Corollary 2.9.7 with the bound.)
Proof, by strong induction on k.
- Base, k = 0: the walk is the single vertex u = v, which is a path of length 0.
- Step. Assume the claim for every walk shorter than k, and let u_0, ..., u_k be a walk with k >= 1. If its vertices are pairwise distinct it is a path of length k, done. Otherwise u_i = u_j for some i < j. Delete u_{i+1}, ..., u_j. The result u_0, ..., u_i, u_{j+1}, ..., u_k is still a walk: the new neighbouring pair is u_i, u_{j+1} = u_j, u_{j+1}, which are adjacent because u_j, u_{j+1} are (if j = k nothing follows u_i and the walk simply ends at u_i = u_k = v). It has the same ends, length k - (j - i) < k, and uses vertices of the original walk in order. By the induction hypothesis it contains a path from u to v of length at most k - (j - i) <= k. QED.
Check by script (part 3): all 101662 walks of length up to 7 or 8 in 8 small graphs were shortened by this exact cutting rule to a path with the same ends and length at most k; one walk needed at most 4 cuts. Example: (1,3,2,4,3,2,4,3,5) becomes (1,3,5) on Fig. 1.2.5. This check supports but is not the proof.

**Corollary 2.4 (three readings of "path between" agree).** For vertices u, v the following are equivalent: (a) there is a walk from u to v; (b) there is a trail from u to v; (c) there is a path from u to v. (M2-7.) Proof: (c) implies (b) implies (a) by Proposition 2.2; (a) implies (c) by Lemma 2.3. QED. Consequence: textbook Definition 2.1.1 (connected, using the book's "path" = trail), the board's wording, and the reading with simple paths define the same class of graphs. (Grinberg's Proposition 2.9.10 covers walks and paths only; the trail step is the paper's.) Script part 5 checks this on all ordered pairs of the 8 graphs.

**Proposition 2.5 (distance).** (a) The length of a shortest walk, a shortest trail and a shortest path from u to v are equal when any exists. (b) So d(u,v) = +infinity exactly when there is no walk from u to v. (c) Every path in a graph with N vertices has length at most N - 1; so a finite d(u,v) is at most N - 1. (M2-8; the bound in (c) is C18.)
Proof. (a) Let k be the length of a shortest walk. By Lemma 2.3 there is a path of length at most k, so exactly k by minimality. A path is a trail and a trail is a walk (Proposition 2.2), so the shortest path, trail and walk lengths are squeezed between k and k. (b) follows from Corollary 2.4. (c) A path of length k has k + 1 pairwise distinct vertices, and there are only N vertices, so k + 1 <= N. QED. Script part 4 finds longest path length N - 1 on the connected graphs tested and at most N - 1 on all.
Remark: the textbook defines d through its "path" (a trail) in 1.2.16; by (a) the value is the same as with simple paths.

**Proposition 2.6 (cycles in a simple graph).** A simple cycle has length at least 3. (M2-9.) Proof: length 1 would need a loop; length 2 would be u, v, u with edges uv and vu, the same edge twice, contradicting the distinct edges required by Definition 1.2.12. The script finds no cycle of length 1 or 2 in any of the 8 graphs. QED. Consistent with the book writing C_n for n >= 3.

## 6. Assumptions and their reasons

1. Finite simple undirected graph (brief, section 7): stated in the user's text, matches the symmetric zero-diagonal matrix.
2. "Path" means no repeated vertex (user's statement, textbook 1.2.13). The reason to adopt it: it is also the standard English meaning, and the lemma and bound in m3 are about this notion.
3. Length-0 walks are allowed (M2-2). Reason: the identity matrix A^0 counts them in m1, and the lemma then also covers u = v. Cost: the textbook is silent on this point.
4. The edge list of Fig. 1.2.5 is read from the page image (M2-5). Reason: the book prints no list; the book's own examples hold on this reading.
5. Names: board (G, V) and e_{ij} in V are the textbook's (V, E); vertices are n_i, the textbook's v_i appears only when citing its figure.

## 7. Limitations, failure cases, competing explanations

- Competing conventions are real: some authors call a trail a "path" (the textbook does) and some English texts call a closed walk a "circuit" or "cycle". Any quoted statement from another source must be translated through Table 1. Paramadevan and Sotheeswaran's Theorem 1 (C6) is an example of a statement that goes wrong when "path" is read in the book's or the English sense: it counts walks.
- The convention for k = 0 is not in the textbook. A reader checking against the book alone may object that Lemma 1.2.1 assumes u different from v; Lemma 2.3 extends it (Grinberg Corollary 2.9.7 has u = v).
- The textbook defines "cycle" as a closed trail and "simple cycle" with "only first and last vertices repeat"; it does not exclude length 0 in the wording. Proposition 2.6 excludes lengths 1 and 2 only. The length-0 closed walk is a closed trail under M2-2; the paper does not call it a cycle, and that choice is the paper's, not the book's.
- Script checks are finite (8 graphs, lengths up to 7 or 8) and are a consistency check on the proofs, not a replacement for them.
- Grinberg's text is a long-lived arXiv lecture-notes version; Wikipedia is tertiary. Neither is peer reviewed; the proofs here do not depend on either, they only give the naming and a second statement of the lemma.
