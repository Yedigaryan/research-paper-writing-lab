# Module m1: Adjacency matrix and walk counting

Register: study-note. Claim ids M1-n are in `evidence/modules/m1.json`; C-n
ids are in the merged `evidence/evidence.json`. Kinds of statement are
named in the text: established fact, derived result, interpretation,
assumption.

## 1. Purpose

Serves question (1) of the brief: what does the entry (A^k)_{ij} represent.
The module establishes: the definition and basic properties of the
adjacency matrix A of a simple undirected graph; the theorem that
(A^k)_{ij} is the number of walks of length k from n_i to n_j, with a full
induction proof; and the warning that this counts walks, not paths, with
counterexamples.

## 2. Prerequisites

- Graph, vertex, edge, adjacent: textbook G = (V, E), Definitions 1.1.1 and
  1.1.4 (printed pp. 9, 11). The board writes (G, V) for the textbook's
  (V, E) and e_{ij} in V for e_{ij} in E (brief, section 7). Not owned by
  any module; the paper's notation list covers them.
- Simple graph: no loops (an edge from a vertex to itself) and no multiple
  edges (two edges with the same two ends). Assumption of the brief.
- Matrix product: (XY)_{ij} = sum over t of X_{it} Y_{tj}. Assumed known;
  stated once so the proof below is self-contained. A^0 is the identity
  matrix I (1 on the diagonal, 0 elsewhere); A^{k+1} = A^k A.
- Walk, length of a walk: owned by m2 (textbook Definition 1.2.10). The
  working form used here is restated in 3.2. Path (no repeated vertices,
  textbook Definition 1.2.13 "simple path"): owned by m2.
- Degree d(n_i), the number of edges at n_i: textbook Definition 1.2.1
  (printed p. 16). The textbook places it after the adjacency matrix.

## 3. Explanation from first principles

### 3.1 The adjacency matrix (established fact: M1-1)

In plain words: a table with one row and one column for each vertex. A 1
says the two vertices share an edge; a 0 says they do not.

Let G be a finite simple undirected graph with N vertices n_1, ..., n_N.
Its adjacency matrix A is the N by N matrix with A_{ij} = 1 if n_i and n_j
are adjacent and A_{ij} = 0 otherwise. This is the textbook's A(G) (printed
p. 11, after Definition 1.1.6, which has no number of its own) and the
board's A_{ij} (whiteboard 1, top line). Remark: only presence of an edge is
recorded, never how many; for a simple graph that loses nothing. Example:
the board matrix, rows 0 1 0 / 1 0 1 / 0 1 0, is the graph with edges
n_1n_2 and n_2n_3. Non-example: the matrix with rows 1 1 0 / 1 0 0 / 0 0 0
is not the adjacency matrix of a simple graph, since A_{11} = 1 would be a
loop.

Board mismatch (C28, fact): the drawing shows three lines (1-2, 1-3, 2-3),
the matrix encodes two. By the brief's assumption the matrix is the example
graph. Calling the three-edge graph "the triangle", section 5 also treats
the triangle, as the graph the drawing would give.

### 3.2 Walks in a simple graph (derived: M1-7; definition is m2's)

In plain words: a walk is a trip along roads, where you may pass the same
town or the same road again.

Textbook Definition 1.2.10: a walk of length k from u_0 to u_k is
u_0, e_1, u_1, ..., e_k, u_k with e_i = u_{i-1}u_i; vertices and edges may
repeat (C10). In a simple graph the edge e_i is fixed by its two ends, so
a walk of length k is the same as a vertex sequence u_0, ..., u_k in which
consecutive vertices are adjacent, and the length k counts edges, with
repeats counted each time. Two walks are equal when their vertex sequences
are equal. Convention (assumption): a single vertex is a walk of length 0.
I did not check whether the textbook admits k = 0, so M1-7 is `qualified`;
the convention is needed for A^0 = I, and Grinberg's Theorem 4.5.10 also
includes k = 0.

### 3.3 Basic properties (M1-1, M1-2, M1-4)

1. Entries are 0 or 1 (by definition).
2. Zero diagonal, A_{ii} = 0: a simple graph has no loops (textbook p. 11,
   fact, C2).
3. Symmetric, A_{ij} = A_{ji}: adjacency does not depend on direction
   (textbook p. 11, fact, C2).
4. Every power is symmetric (derived): (A^k)^T = (A^T)^k = A^k, using
   (XY)^T = Y^T X^T.
5. Row sums are degrees (derived): the sum over j of A_{ij} counts the
   vertices adjacent to n_i. In a simple graph neighbours and edges at n_i
   correspond one to one (no loops, no multiple edges), so the sum is
   d(n_i). By symmetry the column sums are the same. On the board matrix
   the row sums are 1, 2, 1.
6. (A^2)_{ii} = d(n_i) (derived): (A^2)_{ii} = sum over t of A_{it} A_{ti}
   = sum over t of A_{it}^2 = sum over t of A_{it} (symmetry, and 0 or 1
   squared is itself) = d(n_i). Paramadevan and Sotheeswaran state the same
   in the proof of their Theorem 1 (p. 17). Section 3.4 gives a second
   reading of it.

Property 5 is stated for the textbook's degree (Definition 1.2.1). The
textbook does not state properties 4 to 6, so they are the paper's own.

### 3.4 Walk-counting theorem (derived: M1-3)

In plain words: multiply the table by itself k times. Each number in the
result tells how many trips of exactly k steps lead from one town to
another. Trips may revisit towns and roads. Repeated visits are counted.

Theorem 1. Let G be a simple undirected graph with vertices n_1, ..., n_N
and adjacency matrix A. For every integer k >= 0 and all i, j in
{1, ..., N}, (A^k)_{ij} equals W_k(i, j), the number of walks of length k
from n_i to n_j.

The textbook has no such theorem (full-text search, C9, `qualified`: an
absence result from the text layer). Source for the statement:
Grinberg, Theorem 4.5.10, for multidigraphs (C4); Bai's notes state it for
simple graphs (graph-sources.md; not in the evidence file, so not relied
on). The proof below is written for simple graphs and is the paper's own.

Proof, by induction on k. By 3.2, W_k(i, j) is the number of vertex
sequences u_0, ..., u_k with u_0 = n_i, u_k = n_j and u_{s-1} adjacent to
u_s for 1 <= s <= k.

Base case, k = 0. The only walk of length 0 starting at n_i is the single
vertex n_i. So W_0(i, j) = 1 if i = j and 0 otherwise, which is I_{ij} =
(A^0)_{ij}. (For k = 1 directly: a walk of length 1 from n_i to n_j is one
edge, so W_1(i, j) = A_{ij}.)

Induction hypothesis. For some k >= 0, W_k(i, t) = (A^k)_{it} for all
i, t.

Step. Take a walk of length k+1 from n_i to n_j, u_0, ..., u_{k+1}. Let
n_t = u_k be its next-to-last vertex. Dropping the last vertex leaves a walk
of length k from n_i to n_t, and n_t is adjacent to n_j. Conversely, any
walk of length k from n_i to n_t followed by n_j, where n_t is adjacent to
n_j, is a walk of length k+1 from n_i to n_j. So walks of length k+1 from
n_i to n_j are in one to one correspondence with pairs (walk of length k
from n_i to n_t, adjacency of n_t and n_j), for t = 1, ..., N. Different t
give different walks, because t is read off the walk. Hence
W_{k+1}(i, j) = sum over t of W_k(i, t) A_{tj}, since A_{tj} is 1 when n_t
and n_j are adjacent (one allowed last step) and 0 otherwise (none). By the
hypothesis this is the sum over t of (A^k)_{it} A_{tj} = (A^{k+1})_{ij}.
This closes the induction. QED

Why it is valid: the matrix product sums over the intermediate vertex t, and
a walk is built by choosing intermediate vertices one by one. The proof
uses nothing about the graph other than that A records adjacency, so no
condition on connectivity is required. The proof does not use that G is
simple except to identify a walk with its vertex sequence and A with 0 and
1; for a multigraph the same step works with A_{tj} equal to the number of
edges.

Remark (property 6 again): for k = 2 and i = j the theorem says (A^2)_{ii}
counts closed walks n_i, n_t, n_i, one for each neighbour n_t, so it is
d(n_i).

Example (board matrix, section 5): (A^2)_{13} = 1, the walk n_1, n_2, n_3.
Non-example of the claim "the entry is 1 when an edge exists": (A^2)_{12}
= 0, although n_1 and n_2 are adjacent; there is no walk of length exactly
2 from n_1 to n_2, because the graph has no triangle. Exact length is what
matters.

### 3.5 Walks are not paths (derived: M1-5)

In plain words: the table counts trips, and a trip may go to a town and come
straight back. A path is a trip that never visits a town twice. So the table
can show a number where no path exists.

Course convention (textbook Definition 1.2.13, m2): a path has pairwise
distinct vertices. Textbook Definition 1.2.12 calls a walk with distinct
edges "path"; that is a trail, and it is not the course's path. Theorem 1
counts all walks, so it counts neither paths nor trails only.

Counterexamples, on the board graph (edges n_1n_2, n_2n_3):

- (A^2)_{11} = 1 counts the walk n_1, n_2, n_1. It repeats n_1, so it is
  not a path. No path of length 2 from n_1 to n_1 exists (a path from a
  vertex to itself has length 0). In general (A^2)_{ii} = d(n_i) > 0
  whenever n_i has a neighbour.
- (A^3)_{12} = 2 counts n_1, n_2, n_1, n_2 and n_1, n_2, n_3, n_2. Neither
  is a path. The graph has 3 vertices, and a path of length k has k+1 distinct
  vertices (C18), so no path of length 3 exists at all.
- It is also not a trail count: n_1, n_2, n_1 reuses the edge n_1n_2, so it
  is not a textbook 1.2.12 walk either.

So Paramadevan and Sotheeswaran, Theorem 1 (p. 16, "different paths", C6)
is false as written; the entry counts walks. Contradiction recorded in the
merged file between C6 and C5; resolution: the paper proves walks and
cites that source only as an example of the confusion.

What can be said about paths: a positive entry (A^k)_{ij} gives a walk of
length k, hence (m2, C17) a path of length at most k when i differs from j.
That step is m2's lemma and is used by m3. The entry's value does not give
the number of paths.

  used only in 3.5 and defined by m2.

## 7. Limitations, failure cases, competing explanations

- Theorem 1 and its proof are for simple graphs. For a graph with loops or
  multiple edges A has entries other than 0 and 1 and the same proof holds
  with A_{tj} as the edge count; a loop in an undirected graph adds
  conventions (whether it counts once or twice on the diagonal) that are out
  of scope.
- Directed graphs: A is not symmetric; the same proof holds (Grinberg's
  setting) but the properties in 3.3 items 3 to 5 change. One sentence in the
  paper, per scope.
- The theorem counts walks of exactly length k. Counting walks of length
  at most k needs the sum I + A + ... + A^k (this is m3's use).
- Large entries: the numbers grow like powers of the largest degree, so
  the entry is no measure of "how connected" two vertices are.
- Competing statement: Paramadevan and Sotheeswaran say "paths" (false as
  written). The textbook's own word "path" (Def. 1.2.12) would make the
  sentence "counts paths" false under a different reading as well (the
  example n_1, n_2, n_1 repeats an edge).
- The absence of the theorem in the textbook rests on a text-layer search
  (C9), so it is `qualified`.
