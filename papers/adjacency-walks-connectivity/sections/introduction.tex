\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{The questions and where they come from}

This note starts from a lecture whiteboard, photographed twice from slightly
different angles \cite{whiteboard1,whiteboard2}. The board shows a graph with
$\N$ vertices that is given through its adjacency matrix $A$, and it asks two
questions about $A$ \cite{whiteboard1}. In the first sentences a vertex is a point (a town in the story below) and an
edge is a line joining two points (a road); \cref{tab:dictionary} translates every
term. The matrix $A$ is a table of zeros and
ones that records which vertices are joined by an edge (\cref{def:adjmatrix}).
The first question is what the entry in row $i$ and column $j$ of the $k$-th
power of $A$ represents; the $k$-th power is $A$ multiplied by itself $k$ times.
The second question is how to know from $A$ whether the graph is connected. The board then defines a connected
graph as one in which, for any two vertices, there exists a path between them
\cite{whiteboard1}. It does not say what a path is. The assignment text that
came with the photos adds a third question: what is the definition of a path
between two vertices $n_i$ and $n_j$? The assignment text also states that in the course a path is a walk with no
repeated vertex; \cref{conv:path} records this. We call the
three questions Q1, Q2 and Q3:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=2.2em,labelwidth=2em]
  \item[Q1] What does the entry $(A^k)_{ij}$ represent?
  \item[Q2] How can one determine from $A$ whether the graph is connected?
  \item[Q3] What is the definition of a path between $n_i$ and $n_j$?
\end{description}

\noindent The board also holds a small drawing of three vertices and a
$3\times 3$ matrix (\cref{fig:boardredraw}). In that figure the board's $V$ stands for the edge set
$E$, and the small mark above $A$ (drawn as $?$) is read as the exponent $k$
(\cref{conv:edgeset,conv:power}). We use that matrix as the main
example, and \cref{sec:board-mismatch} reports where the drawing and the matrix
differ.

\subsection{The story used throughout}

\plain
Think of a group of towns. Some pairs of towns have a road between them.
Every road can be driven in both directions. A trip follows roads, one after
another. You may drive past the same town again, or the same road again.
Two questions come up. How many different trips with a given number of drives
lead from one town to another? And can you get from every town to every other town?

The road table has one row and one column for each town. It has a one where the
row town and the column town share a road, and a zero otherwise. For three
towns in a row, where the first town has a road to the second and the second has
a road to the third, the road table is the board's matrix
$\matBoardA$. \Cref{tab:dictionary} fixes the story words once. The plain words paragraphs
in this note use only the story words in the right column. The technical
terms in the left column are defined where they first appear.

\subsection{What the note shows}

The short answers are as follows. The sections named give the definitions and
proofs.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item \textbf{Q1.} For $k\ge 1$, the entry $(A^k)_{ij}$ is the number of
  \emph{walks} of length $k$ from $n_i$ to $n_j$ (\cref{thm:walkcount}). It
  counts walks, not paths; \cref{sec:notpaths} shows by example why the
  difference matters.
  \item \textbf{Q2.} The graph on $\N\ge 2$ vertices is connected if and only
  if every off-diagonal entry of $A+A^2+\dots+A^{\N-1}$ is positive, and this
  holds if and only if $(I+A)^{\N-1}$ has no zero entry (\cref{thm:test}). The
  exponent $\N-1$ is the longest a path can be, and adding $I$ lets one power stand
  in for the sum (\cref{sec:test}).
  Here $I$ is the identity matrix (ones on the diagonal, zeros elsewhere), and an
  off-diagonal entry is an entry whose row number differs from its column
  number.
  \item \textbf{Q3.} Under the course convention, a path between $n_i$ and
  $n_j$ is a sequence of pairwise distinct vertices that starts at $n_i$, ends
  at $n_j$, and has adjacent vertices in each consecutive pair
  (\cref{def:pathij}).
\end{itemize}

The course textbook defines the graph, the adjacency matrix, walks, paths,
cycles, distance and connectivity \cite{petrosyan2015graph}. As far as a search
of the book shows (the claim covers only the pages found by that search and the
pages viewed), it does not state the walk-counting theorem or the matrix test
for connectivity; \cref{app:provenance} says what was searched and which pages
were viewed \cite{petrosyan2015graph}. Both results are therefore
proved here in full. Lecture notes state them as well
\cite{galwaynotes,bai2025adjacency,gotti18211lecture27}, and we name the source
beside each statement.

\subsection{How the note is organized}

\Cref{sec:conventions} fixes notation and the conventions we adopt where the
board, the assignment text and the textbook leave a choice. \Cref{sec:background}
gives the definitions in the textbook's order, each followed by a remark, an

propositions are proved here unless a source is named. A reading of the photos or
of the assignment text is a numbered convention in \cref{sec:conventions}.
