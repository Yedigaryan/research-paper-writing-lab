\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{The three questions and where they came from}

This note replies to a student's photographs of a lecture board and to the
text of an assignment. Everything on the board that matters is reproduced
here, so the reader needs no photograph. The board shows a small drawing with
three points labelled 1, 2 and 3, and next to it a table of zeros and ones
with the rows $0\,1\,0$, $1\,0\,1$ and $0\,1\,0$
\cite{whiteboard1lecture,whiteboard2lecture}. It says that a graph with $N$
nodes is given through this table, called its adjacency matrix and written
$A$, and it asks two questions \cite{whiteboard1lecture}. A third question
comes from the assignment.

\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textbf{Q1}] (board, question 1) What does the entry in row $i$ and
  column $j$ of the $k$-th power $A^k$ represent?
\item[\textbf{Q2}] (board, question 2) How can one tell from $A$ whether
  the graph is connected?
\item[\textbf{Q3}] (assignment) What is the definition of a path between two
  vertices $n_i$ and $n_j$? The board uses the word \emph{path} in its
  definition of connected but does not define it \cite{whiteboard1lecture}.
\end{enumerate}

Short answers, with every word defined in \cref{sec:background}. Q1: the entry counts the walks of exactly $k$ roads from
$n_i$ to $n_j$ (\cref{thm:count}); it counts walks, not paths
(\cref{prop:notpaths}). Q2: the graph is connected exactly when a sum of
powers of $A$ has no zero off the diagonal (\cref{thm:test}). Q3: a path is
a walk that never visits a vertex twice (\cref{def:path}). The order of the
note follows what each answer needs. The words come first (\cref{sec:background}),
then the comparison of path conventions (\cref{sec:paths}), then Q1
(\cref{sec:counting}) and Q2 (\cref{sec:connectivity}), and finally the limits
(\cref{sec:discussion}) and the conclusion (\cref{sec:conclusion}).

\subsection{The story used in the plain-words paragraphs}

Before every definition, theorem and worked example there is a short
story paragraph, marked in bold at its start. The story is this. A country
has towns and roads. Each road joins two different towns. Two towns share at
most one road. People make trips along the roads. \Cref{fig:three} shows the
country of the board. It has three towns in a row. A road joins towns 1 and
2, and a road joins towns 2 and 3. The story paragraphs use only the story

\subsection{Conventions and assumptions}\label{sec:conventions}

The board and the textbook differ in places, and the board has one mark that
cannot be read with certainty. We fix the following conventions. They are
cited later by their number.

\begin{enumerate}
\item\label{conv:graph} \emph{Finite simple undirected graphs.} Every graph
  is finite, has no road from a town to itself, no two roads between the same
  two towns, and no one-way roads. The assignment states this setting, and the
  symmetric, zero-diagonal matrix on the board fits it
  \cite{whiteboard1lecture}.
\item\label{conv:power} \emph{The exponent in Q1 is $k$.} On the board, question 1
  has a small mark above the letter $A$. It is a small letter between
  quotation-like strokes that could be read as $k$ or $n$
  \cite{whiteboard1lecture,whiteboard2lecture}. The assignment asks about
  powers $A^k$, so we read $k$. This is an assumption, not something read off
  the board.
\item\label{conv:matrix} \emph{The matrix, not the drawing, is the example.}
  The drawing on the board has three lines, between towns 1 and 2 (with an
  arrowhead at 2), between 1 and 3, and between 2 and 3; \cref{fig:three}(b)
  shows it. The matrix has a $1$ only for the pairs $(1,2)$ and $(2,3)$
  \cite{whiteboard1lecture,whiteboard2lecture}. So the drawing has an extra
  line between 1 and 3 that the matrix does not have. The problem statement says the graph is given through its matrix,
  so \cref{fig:three}(a) is the example, and the arrowhead is read as a stray
  mark because the matrix is symmetric. The drawing's three-road map
  (\cref{fig:three}(b)) is also computed, as a side example.
\item\label{conv:notation} \emph{Notation.} The board writes $(G,V)$ for a
  graph, and defines $A_{ij}=1$ if $e_{ij}\in V$ and $A_{ij}=0$ if
  $e_{ij}\notin V$. Here $e_{ij}$ is the edge between $n_i$ and $n_j$. The
  textbook writes $G=(V,E)$ with $V$ the set of vertices and $E$ the set of
  edges. So the board uses $V$ for the set of edges where the textbook uses
  $E$ \cite{whiteboard1lecture,petrosyan2015grafneri}. We write $G=(V,E)$,
  $V=\{n_1,\dots,n_N\}$, $N=|V|$, and $e_{ij}$ or $n_in_j$ for the edge
  joining $n_i$ and $n_j$. The textbook calls the vertices $v_i$; this note
  uses $n_i$ as on the board.
  The textbook's page numbers in this note are printed page numbers, which
  are one less than the page numbers of the PDF file.
\item\label{conv:path} \emph{The word ``path''.} Under the course
  convention, given with the assignment, a path has no repeated vertex. This is
  the textbook's \emph{simple path}. The textbook's own word ``path'' means
  something weaker. \Cref{tab:conventions} compares the two.
\item\label{conv:zero} \emph{Walks of length 0.} We allow a walk that is
  one vertex and no edge. The textbook's definition does not say whether
  this is allowed \cite{petrosyan2015grafneri}; the choice is ours, made so
  that $A^0$, the identity matrix, also counts walks.
\item\label{conv:fig} \emph{The five-town map.} Several examples use the map
  of \cref{fig:five}. It is drawn after the textbook's Fig.\ 1.2.5
  \cite[p.~21]{petrosyan2015grafneri}. The textbook prints no list of
  edges, so we read one from the page image. The list is given in the caption
  of \cref{fig:five}, and the examples need only that list, not the textbook's
  figure. The textbook's own examples of a walk, a trail, a path and a cycle
  on that figure come out as the book says on this list.
\end{enumerate}

The notation used throughout is as follows. $A=(A_{ij})$ is the adjacency
matrix; $A^k$ is its $k$-th power and $(A^k)_{ij}$ the entry in row $i$ and
column $j$ of that power; $I$ is the identity matrix; $\deg(n_i)$ is the degree
of $n_i$, and $d(u,v)$, with two arguments, is the distance between $u$ and $v$; $S=A+A^2+\dots+A^{N-1}$ is the sum used in the test of
\cref{sec:connectivity}. The letters $N$ (number of vertices), $k$ (length
of a walk or exponent), $i,j,t$ (indices) and $S$ keep one meaning
throughout.

\subsection{What the sources contain}\label{sec:sources}

The textbook \cite{petrosyan2015grafneri} defines the adjacency matrix, walks,
trails, paths, cycles, distance and connectedness, and we use its numbers. We
searched the textbook's text for the word ``matrix'' and did not find a theorem
about the powers of $A$ or a matrix test for connectivity. This shows only
that we did not find them, so we prove both results in this note.
Grinberg's lecture notes \cite{grinberg2025introduction} state the
walk-counting theorem for multidigraphs, which are more general than simple
graphs, and give the walk-to-path lemma with a length bound. Paramadevan and
Sotheeswaran \cite{paramadevan2021properties} state the connectivity test as
their Theorem~2. The details of what was read, and where, are in
\cref{app:provenance}; our comments on their Theorems~1 and 2 are in
\cref{sec:discussion}.
