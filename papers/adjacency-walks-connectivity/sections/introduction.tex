\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

\subsection{The questions and where they come from}

This note starts from a lecture whiteboard, photographed twice from slightly
different angles \cite{whiteboard1,whiteboard2}. The board shows a graph with
$\N$ vertices that is given through its adjacency matrix $A$, and it asks two
questions about $A$ \cite{whiteboard1}. The first question is what the entry
$(i,j)$ of a power of $A$ represents. The second question is how to know
from $A$ whether the graph is connected. The board then defines a connected
graph as one in which, for any two vertices, there exists a path between them
\cite{whiteboard1}. It does not say what a path is. The assignment text that
came with the photos adds a third question: what is the definition of a path
between two vertices $n_i$ and $n_j$? The assignment text also states which
meaning of the word path the course uses; \cref{conv:path} records it. We call the
three questions Q1, Q2 and Q3:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=2.2em,labelwidth=2em]
  \item[Q1] What does the entry $(A^k)_{ij}$ represent?
  \item[Q2] How can one determine from $A$ whether the graph is connected?
  \item[Q3] What is the definition of a path between $n_i$ and $n_j$?
\end{description}

\noindent The board also holds a small drawing of three vertices and a
$3\times 3$ matrix. We use that matrix as the main example, and
\cref{sec:board-mismatch} reports where the drawing and the matrix differ.

\subsection{The story used throughout}

\plain
Think of a group of towns. Some pairs of towns have a road between them.
Every road can be driven in both directions. A trip follows roads, one after
another. You may drive past the same town again, or the same road again.
Two questions come up. How many different trips of a given size lead from one
town to another? And can you get from every town to every other town?

\Cref{tab:dictionary} fixes the story words once. The plain words paragraphs
in this note use only the story words in the right column. The technical
terms in the left column are defined where they first appear.

\subsection{What the note shows}

The short answers are as follows. The sections named give the definitions and
proofs.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
  \item \textbf{Q1.} For $k\ge 1$, the entry $(A^k)_{ij}$ is the number of
  \emph{walks} of length $k$ from $n_i$ to $n_j$ (\cref{thm:walkcount}). It
  counts walks, not paths; \cref{sec:notpaths} shows by example why the
  difference matters.
  \item \textbf{Q2.} The graph on $\N\ge 2$ vertices is connected if and only
  if every off-diagonal entry of $A+A^2+\dots+A^{\N-1}$ is positive, and this
  holds if and only if $(I+A)^{\N-1}$ has no zero entry (\cref{thm:test}).
  \item \textbf{Q3.} Under the course convention, a path between $n_i$ and
  $n_j$ is a sequence of pairwise distinct vertices that starts at $n_i$, ends
  at $n_j$, and has adjacent vertices in each consecutive pair
  (\cref{def:pathij}).
\end{itemize}

The course textbook defines the graph, the adjacency matrix, walks, paths,
cycles, distance and connectivity \cite{petrosyan2015graph}. As far as a text
search of the book for the adjacency matrix shows, it mentions that matrix on
the table of contents and on printed pages 7, 11, 12, 34 and 61, and the pages
we viewed contain neither a theorem that counts walks with matrix powers nor a
matrix test for connectivity \cite{petrosyan2015graph}. We did not read the
whole book, so we state this only as the result of that search. Both results
are therefore proved here in full. Lecture notes state them as well
\cite{galwaynotes,bai2025adjacency,gotti18211lecture27}, and we name the
source beside each statement.

\subsection{How the note is organized}

\Cref{sec:conventions} fixes notation and the conventions we adopt where the
board, the assignment text and the textbook leave a choice. \Cref{sec:background}
gives the definitions in the textbook's order, each followed by a remark, an
example and a non-example. \Cref{sec:paths} answers Q3 and proves the lemmas
about walks and paths that the other answers need. \Cref{sec:walkcount}
answers Q1. \Cref{sec:connectivity} answers Q2 and works two examples.
\Cref{sec:discussion} states limits, and \cref{sec:conclusion} gives the direct
answers. \Cref{app:provenance} lists the sources, the pages viewed and how to
rerun every computation.

Statements are marked by their form, not by labels. A definition carries the
textbook's number where it has one. A theorem, lemma or proposition that has a
proof printed here is our own proof; a citation in its first sentence says which
source also states the result. A reading of the photos or of the assignment
text is a numbered convention in \cref{sec:conventions}.
