\section{Worked examples}\label{sec:example}

Every matrix in this section was produced by a script and is read into the text from a generated file, so that none was typed by hand (\cref{app:repro}).
The example graph is $B$, the graph of the board matrix.
The board drawing differs from the board matrix by one edge $n_1n_3$ and an arrowhead (\cref{sec:question}); the matrix is followed.

\subsection{The powers of the board matrix}\label{sec:powers}

\plainwords We take the village from the board.
It has three houses in a row.
The first and last houses each have one road.
The middle house has two roads.
We count trips of two roads, then trips of three roads.
We also list every trip, so the reader can see that each count is right.

\begin{example}[powers of the board matrix]\label{ex:boardpowers}
For $B$ the matrix and its first powers are
\[
A=\MBA,\qquad A^2=\MBAtwo,\qquad A^3=\MBAthree .
\]
Also $A^4=\MBAfour$ and $A^5=\MBAfive$, and $A^3=2A$.
The diagonal of $A^2$ is $(1,2,1)$, the degrees of $n_1,n_2,n_3$, and its sum is $4=2|E|$.
\end{example}

The walks behind these entries, with vertices written by index, are the following.
\begin{itemize}
\item Length $1$: $1\text{-}2$ and $2\text{-}3$, and their reverses.
\item Length $2$: from $n_1$ to $n_1$ only $1\text{-}2\text{-}1$ (one walk); from $n_1$ to $n_3$ only $1\text{-}2\text{-}3$ (one); from $n_2$ to $n_2$ the walks $2\text{-}1\text{-}2$ and $2\text{-}3\text{-}2$ (two); from $n_3$ to $n_3$ only $3\text{-}2\text{-}3$ (one).
\item Length $3$: from $n_1$ to $n_2$ the walks $1\text{-}2\text{-}1\text{-}2$ and $1\text{-}2\text{-}3\text{-}2$ (two); from $n_2$ to $n_3$ the walks $2\text{-}1\text{-}2\text{-}3$ and $2\text{-}3\text{-}2\text{-}3$ (two).
\end{itemize}
Each count equals the matrix entry in that position, and the script asserts this for every entry of $A$, $A^2$, and $A^3$.
The sums of the induction step of \cref{thm:walkcount} can be checked by hand:
\begin{align*}
(A^2)_{22}&=A_{21}A_{12}+A_{22}A_{22}+A_{23}A_{32}=1\cdot1+0\cdot0+1\cdot1=2,\\
(A^3)_{12}&=(A^2)_{11}A_{12}+(A^2)_{12}A_{22}+(A^2)_{13}A_{32}=1\cdot1+0\cdot0+1\cdot1=2.
\end{align*}
In the second line the term for $r=1$ is the length-$2$ walk $1\text{-}2\text{-}1$ extended by the step to $n_2$, which gives $1\text{-}2\text{-}1\text{-}2$, and the term for $r=3$ is the walk $1\text{-}2\text{-}3$ extended by the step to $n_2$, which gives $1\text{-}2\text{-}3\text{-}2$.

\subsection{A connected example}\label{sec:testB}

\plainwords Take three houses in a row.
A road joins the first and the second house.
Another road joins the second and the third.
The first and the third share no road.
Can you still go from one to the other?
Yes, through the middle house.
We expect the check to say connected.

\begin{example}[connected: the board matrix]\label{ex:testB}
$B$ has edges $n_1n_2$ and $n_2n_3$, $N=3$, and $N-1=2$.
\[
A=\MBA,\quad A^2=\MBAtwo,\quad S=A+A^2=\MBS,\quad (I+A)^2=\MBI .
\]
$S$ has no zero entry, so by \cref{thm:test}(b) $G$ is connected, and $(I+A)^2$ has no zero entry, so by \cref{thm:test}(c) it is connected: the two answers agree.
The $(1,3)$ entry of $A$ is $0$, so no road joins $n_1$ and $n_3$, yet $(A^2)_{13}=1$: there is exactly one walk of length $2$ from $n_1$ to $n_3$, namely $1\text{-}2\text{-}3$.
The $(1,3)$ entry of $(I+A)^2$ is $1=\binom22(A^2)_{13}$, since the other terms of the expansion vanish there.
The identity $(I+A)^2=I+2A+A^2$ holds entry by entry, by script.
Breadth-first search from $n_1$ reaches $\{1,2,3\}$, which agrees.
\end{example}

\subsection{A disconnected example}\label{sec:testD}

\plainwords Now take three houses again.
Only one road exists, between the first and the second house.
The third house stands alone.
Nobody can walk to it.
We expect the check to say not connected.
It should also show that the third house is a piece by itself.

\begin{example}[disconnected: one edge only]\label{ex:testD}
$D$ has the single edge $n_1n_2$, $N=3$, and $N-1=2$.
\[
A=\MDA,\quad A^2=\MDAtwo,\quad S=A+A^2=\MDS,\quad (I+A)^2=\MDI .
\]
The off-diagonal zeros of $S$ are at $(1,3)$, $(2,3)$, $(3,1)$, and $(3,2)$, and the zeros of $(I+A)^2$ are at the same four places.
So by \cref{thm:test} the graph is not connected, and by \cref{cor:components} the vertices $n_1$ and $n_2$ lie in one component and $n_3$ in another: the components are $\{n_1,n_2\}$ and $\{n_3\}$, which agrees with breadth-first search.
Higher powers do not help: the script checked that the entries $(1,3)$ and $(2,3)$ of $A^k$ are $0$ for $k=1,\dots,6$, and in fact no walk reaches $n_3$ at all.
\end{example}

The example with $N-1$ replaced by $N-2$ is \cref{ex:sharp}.

\subsection{Agreement with breadth-first search on all small graphs}\label{sec:exhaustive}

\plainwords We can also try the check on every possible small village.
We compare its answer with the marking method.
They agree every time.
This does not replace the proof, but it would reveal a slip.

By computer, the test of \cref{thm:test}(b), the form (c), the statement of \cref{cor:components}, and the version of (c) with repeated squaring (\cref{sec:cost}) were compared with an independent breadth-first search on every labelled simple graph with $N=2,\dots,6$ vertices.
\Cref{tab:exhaustive} gives the counts.
In all $2+8+64+1024+32768$ graphs the four methods agreed with breadth-first search.
The last column shows what goes wrong with $N-2$ powers: it gives the wrong answer on exactly the connected graphs that are labelled paths, which number $N!/2$ for the sizes shown; for $N=2$ the sum with zero powers is empty and is wrong on the one connected graph.
This check covers $N\le 6$ only.
