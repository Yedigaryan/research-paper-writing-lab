\section{Background: graphs, matrices, walks, and paths}\label{sec:background}

This section builds the vocabulary in the order of the textbook \cite{petrosyan2015grafneri}: graph, adjacent vertices, adjacency matrix, degree, walk, closed walk, trail, path, cycle, distance, and connectivity.
Nothing is used before it is defined.
Every definition is followed by a remark on what it allows and forbids, an example, and a non-example.
A definition that comes from the textbook carries the textbook's number.
A proposition, lemma, or corollary whose proof is printed here is this note's own statement of the result; a citation in its first sentence names a source that also states it.
Four small graphs are used for the examples.
\begin{itemize}
\item $B$ is the board graph read from the board matrix: vertices $n_1,n_2,n_3$ and edges $n_1n_2$ and $n_2n_3$.
\item $D$ has vertices $n_1,n_2,n_3$ and the single edge $n_1n_2$; it is the disconnected example.
\item $T$ is the board drawing read as drawn: vertices $n_1,n_2,n_3$ and edges $n_1n_2$, $n_1n_3$, $n_2n_3$, a triangle.
It is used only where a graph with a cycle is needed, and it is not the running example.
\item $H$ has vertices $v_1,\dots,v_5$ and the seven edges $v_1v_2$, $v_1v_3$, $v_2v_3$, $v_2v_4$, $v_3v_4$, $v_3v_5$, $v_4v_5$.
These are the edges that the textbook's own examples of walks, paths, and cycles use \cite[p.~22]{petrosyan2015grafneri}; $H$ is used to show differences that $B$ is too small to show.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Graphs, adjacency, and the adjacency matrix}\label{sec:graphs}

\plainwords A village has a list of houses and a list of roads.
A road always joins two different houses.
No two roads join the same pair of houses.
A road can be driven both ways.

\begin{definition}[simple undirected graph; textbook Definition~1.1.1]\label{def:graph}
A \emph{simple undirected graph} is a pair $G=(V,E)$ where $V$ is a finite nonempty set of \emph{vertices} and $E$ is a set of unordered pairs of distinct vertices, the \emph{edges} \cite[Section~1.1]{petrosyan2015grafneri}.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:graph}
The definition forbids a loop (an edge from a vertex to itself, since the two ends of an edge must be distinct) and a repeated edge (since $E$ is a set, a pair occurs at most once).
It forbids direction, since the pair is unordered and $n_in_j$ and $n_jn_i$ are the same edge.
It allows vertices that belong to no edge.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}
$B$ is a simple undirected graph with $V=\{n_1,n_2,n_3\}$ and $E=\{n_1n_2,\,n_2n_3\}$.
\end{example}

\begin{nonexample}
Two vertices joined by two roads repeat an edge, and a road from $n_1$ to $n_1$ is a loop; neither is a simple graph.
\end{nonexample}

\plainwords Two houses are neighbours when one road joins them directly.
Being neighbours does not say anything about longer trips.

\begin{definition}[adjacent vertices; textbook Definition~1.1.4]\label{def:adjacent}
Vertices $u$ and $v$ are \emph{adjacent} if $uv\in E$; then $u$ is a \emph{neighbour} of $v$ \cite[p.~11]{petrosyan2015grafneri}.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
Adjacency is symmetric, because $uv=vu$, and no vertex is adjacent to itself, because there are no loops.
Adjacency says nothing about connections through other vertices.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}
In $B$ the vertices $n_1$ and $n_2$ are adjacent.
\end{example}

\begin{nonexample}
In $B$ the vertices $n_1$ and $n_3$ are not adjacent, although a trip of two roads joins them.
\end{nonexample}

\plainwords Make a square table with one row and one column for every house.
Write a one where the row house and the column house share a road.
Write a zero everywhere else.
The table describes the whole village.

\begin{definition}[adjacency matrix; textbook, after Definition~1.1.6, p.~11]\label{def:adjmatrix}
Let $G=(V,E)$ with $V=\{n_1,\dots,n_N\}$.
The \emph{adjacency matrix} of $G$ is the $N$ by $N$ matrix $A$ whose entry in row $i$ and column $j$ is $A_{ij}=1$ if $n_i$ and $n_j$ are adjacent and $A_{ij}=0$ otherwise \cite[p.~11]{petrosyan2015grafneri}.
\end{definition}

The textbook gives this definition without a number of its own, right after Definition~1.1.6.
The board writes the same rule as $A_{ij}=1$ if $e_{ij}$ belongs to the edge set and $A_{ij}=0$ if it does not \cite{whiteboard1,whiteboard2}.

\begin{remark}
The definition allows only the entries $0$ and $1$.
It depends on how the vertices are numbered: renumbering permutes the rows and the columns together.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}
For $B$ the adjacency matrix is $A=\MBA$, which is the matrix on the board: $A_{12}=A_{21}=1$, $A_{23}=A_{32}=1$, and every other entry is $0$.
\end{example}

\begin{nonexample}
The matrix with rows $(0\,1\,0)$, $(0\,0\,1)$, $(0\,1\,0)$ is not the adjacency matrix of any simple undirected graph, because $A_{12}=1$ but $A_{21}=0$, which would need a one-way road.
A matrix with a one on the diagonal would need a loop, and one with an entry $2$ would need a double road.
\end{nonexample}

\plainwords Later we multiply this table by itself.
We say here exactly what that means.
To fill one place of the new table, take a row of the first table and a column of the second.
Multiply the numbers that sit in the same position, and add the products.

\begin{definition}[matrix product and power]\label{def:product}
For $N$ by $N$ matrices $X$ and $Y$ the \emph{product} $XY$ is the $N$ by $N$ matrix with entries $(XY)_{ij}=\sum_{r=1}^{N}X_{ir}Y_{rj}$.
The \emph{powers} of $A$ are $A^1=A$ and $A^k=A^{k-1}A$ for $k\ge 2$; the \emph{identity matrix} $I$ satisfies $IX=XI=X$ and is used as $A^0=I$ only where stated.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
The product is row times column, and it is a sum of $N$ terms.
It is not commutative in general, but powers of one matrix $A$ commute with each other.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}
For $B$, $(A^2)_{11}=0\cdot0+1\cdot1+0\cdot0=1$.
\end{example}

\begin{nonexample}
Squaring each entry of $A$ separately gives $A$ again, because its entries are $0$ and $1$; this is not $A^2$.
\end{nonexample}

\plainwords The number of roads at a house tells how busy it is.
The table lets us read that number straight off.
Adding one row of the table gives it.

\begin{definition}[degree; textbook Definition~1.2.1]\label{def:degree}
The \emph{degree} $\deg(n_i)$ of a vertex $n_i$ is the number of edges that contain $n_i$ \cite[p.~16]{petrosyan2015grafneri}.
\end{definition}

The textbook writes $d_G(v)$ or $d(v)$ for the degree and defines it after the adjacency matrix, which is why it appears here after \cref{def:adjmatrix}.

\begin{remark}
In a simple graph the degree is also the number of neighbours, since each neighbour is joined to $n_i$ by exactly one edge.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}
In $B$, $\deg(n_1)=1$, $\deg(n_2)=2$, $\deg(n_3)=1$.
\end{example}

\begin{nonexample}
$\deg(n_1)$ is not $2$ in $B$, even though two other vertices can be reached from $n_1$: only edges at $n_1$ count, and $n_3$ is reached by going through $n_2$.
\end{nonexample}

\plainwords The table has easy features.
It looks the same when flipped over its diagonal.
The diagonal holds only zeros.
Adding up one row counts the roads at that house.

\begin{proposition}[properties of $A$; textbook, pp.~11--12]\label{prop:rowsums}
Let $A$ be the adjacency matrix of a simple undirected graph with $N$ vertices and $|E|$ edges.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Every entry of $A$ is $0$ or $1$.
\item $A$ is symmetric: $A_{ij}=A_{ji}$ for all $i,j$.
\item The diagonal is zero: $A_{ii}=0$ for all $i$.
\item The sum of row $i$, and of column $i$, equals $\deg(n_i)$.
\item The sum of all entries equals $2|E|$.
\item Conversely, every $N$ by $N$ matrix of zeros and ones that is symmetric with zero diagonal is the adjacency matrix of a simple undirected graph on $N$ numbered vertices.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

Items 1 to 3 and 6 are stated in the textbook \cite[pp.~11--12]{petrosyan2015grafneri}, item 6 is also stated by Paramadevan and Sotheeswaran \cite{paramadevan2021adjacency}, and item 5 is the textbook's Theorem~1.2.1 (Euler) \cite[p.~16]{petrosyan2015grafneri}.
Item 4 is proved here; Paramadevan and Sotheeswaran state a version of it that allows loops \cite{paramadevan2021adjacency}.

\begin{proof}
Items 1 to 3 are read off \cref{def:adjmatrix} and \cref{def:adjacent}: adjacency is symmetric and no vertex is adjacent to itself.
Item 4: the sum of row $i$ is the number of $j$ with $A_{ij}=1$, which is the number of neighbours of $n_i$.
Each neighbour is joined to $n_i$ by exactly one edge, and no edge at $n_i$ joins it to itself, so this number is the number of edges at $n_i$, that is $\deg(n_i)$.
Column $i$ is the same by symmetry.
Item 5: add item 4 over all $i$; every edge contributes one to the degree of each of its two ends, so it is counted twice.
Item 6: join $n_i$ and $n_j$ exactly when the entry is $1$; symmetry makes this a set of unordered pairs, and the zero diagonal excludes loops.
\end{proof}

For the board matrix the row sums are $1,2,1$, the column sums are $1,2,1$, and the total is $4=2|E|$ with $|E|=2$; these were checked by script (\cref{app:repro}).

\subsection{Walks, trails, paths, and cycles}\label{sec:walkdefs}

\plainwords A trip is a list of houses, one after another.
Each next house must sit at the far end of a road from the house before.
You may pass a house twice.
You may drive a road twice, and then it counts twice.
The length of a trip is the number of roads you drove.
Staying at home is also a trip, of length zero.

\begin{definition}[walk; textbook Definition~1.2.10]\label{def:walk}
A \emph{walk of length $k$ from $u_0$ to $u_k$}, also called a $(u_0,u_k)$-walk, is a sequence $u_0,e_1,u_1,\dots,u_{k-1},e_k,u_k$ of vertices and edges with $e_i=u_{i-1}u_i$ for $1\le i\le k$ \cite[p.~21]{petrosyan2015grafneri}.
The \emph{length} of the walk is $k$, the number of edges, each edge counted as often as it occurs \cite[p.~22]{petrosyan2015grafneri}.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:walk}
Vertices and edges of a walk may repeat \cite[p.~22]{petrosyan2015grafneri}.
The definition forbids only a jump: two consecutive vertices that are not joined by an edge.
A walk of length $k$ lists $k+1$ vertices.
In a simple graph there is at most one edge between two vertices, so the edge $e_i$ is fixed by the vertices $u_{i-1}$ and $u_i$.
Hence a walk is the same thing as its vertex sequence $u_0,u_1,\dots,u_k$ in which every two consecutive vertices are adjacent, and two walks differ exactly when their vertex sequences differ at some position.
The proofs below count such sequences.
The textbook does not say whether $k=0$ is allowed.
This note allows it, as an interpretation, because the textbook's own property $d(u,u)=0$ of the distance (\cref{def:distance}) needs a path of length $0$ from $u$ to $u$ \cite[p.~23]{petrosyan2015grafneri}.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}
In $B$ the sequence $1,2,1,2,3$ is a walk from $n_1$ to $n_3$ of length $4$: its steps $12,21,12,23$ are all edges.
The textbook's example in $H$ is the walk $v_1,v_3,v_2,v_4,v_3,v_2,v_4,v_3,v_5$ of length $8$ \cite[p.~22]{petrosyan2015grafneri}.
\end{example}

\begin{nonexample}
In $B$ the sequence $1,3$ is not a walk, because $n_1n_3$ is not an edge of $B$.
It would be a walk in $T$.
The textbook's non-example is $v_3,v_1,v_4,v_2,v_3,v_5$, which is not a walk of $H$ because $v_1v_4$ is not an edge \cite[p.~22]{petrosyan2015grafneri}.
\end{nonexample}

\plainwords A trip is closed when it ends at the house where it began.
That is the only new rule.

\begin{definition}[closed walk; textbook Definition~1.2.11]\label{def:closed}
A $(u_0,u_k)$-walk is \emph{closed} if $u_0=u_k$ \cite[p.~22]{petrosyan2015grafneri}.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
A closed walk may still repeat vertices and edges.
In a simple graph no closed walk has length $1$, since that would be a loop, and a closed walk of length $2$ is $u,w,u$, which drives one edge twice.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}
In $B$ the walk $2,1,2$ is closed, of length $2$.
\end{example}

\begin{nonexample}
In $B$ the walk $1,2,3$ is not closed: it starts at $n_1$ and ends at $n_3$.
\end{nonexample}

\plainwords Now add one rule.
Never drive the same road twice.
You may still pass a house twice, if you came back by a different road.
English books call this kind of trip a trail.
Our textbook calls it a path.
This note says trail, so that the word path keeps one meaning.

\begin{definition}[trail; textbook Definition~1.2.12, where it is called a ``path'']\label{def:trail}
A $(u_0,u_k)$-walk is a \emph{trail} if its edges $u_0u_1,\dots,u_{k-1}u_k$ are pairwise distinct \cite[p.~22]{petrosyan2015grafneri}.
\end{definition}

The textbook's word for this is ``path''.
In English usage a walk with distinct edges is a trail \cite{grinberg2025graph}.
To point at the textbook's word, this note writes ``textbook path''.

\begin{remark}
A trail allows repeated vertices and forbids repeated edges.
A vertex can recur in a trail only if the trip left it by one road and came back by another.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}
In $H$ the sequence $v_1,v_3,v_2,v_4,v_3,v_5$ is a trail: its edges $v_1v_3,v_3v_2,v_2v_4,v_4v_3,v_3v_5$ are distinct, although $v_3$ occurs twice \cite[p.~22]{petrosyan2015grafneri}.
\end{example}

\begin{nonexample}
In $H$ the length-$8$ walk of the example after \cref{def:walk} is not a trail, because the edge $v_3v_2$ occurs twice \cite[p.~22]{petrosyan2015grafneri}.
In $B$ the walk $1,2,1,2,3$ is not a trail, because the edge $n_1n_2$ occurs twice.
\end{nonexample}

\plainwords Now add a stronger rule.
Never pass the same house twice.
Then you also never drive the same road twice.
A trip like this goes straight from start to end without any loop.
This is what our course calls a path.

\begin{definition}[path; textbook Definition~1.2.13, where it is called a ``simple path'']\label{def:path}
A $(u_0,u_k)$-walk is a \emph{path} if its vertices $u_0,\dots,u_k$ are pairwise distinct \cite[p.~22]{petrosyan2015grafneri}.
When $u_0=u$ and $u_k=v$ it is a \emph{path from $u$ to $v$}, or a \emph{path between $u$ and $v$}.
\end{definition}

The textbook calls this a simple path and defines it as a path in its own sense (a trail) with distinct vertices \cite[p.~22]{petrosyan2015grafneri}.
\Cref{prop:pathtrail} below shows that distinct vertices already give distinct edges, so the two ways of saying it agree.
In English usage a path is a walk with distinct vertices \cite{grinberg2025graph,wikipedia_path_graph_theory}.
That ``path'' means this in the course is stated by the assignment text and is not printed in the textbook; the board does not say which meaning of ``path'' it uses \cite{whiteboard1,whiteboard2}.
This note therefore uses \cref{def:path} for ``path'' everywhere, as an interpretation of the course convention, and \cref{sec:path} compares the conventions.

\begin{remark}
A path forbids repeated vertices and therefore repeated edges (\cref{prop:pathtrail}).
It allows length $0$, a single vertex, and length $1$, a single edge.
It does not allow a closed path of positive length, since the first and last vertex would be equal.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}
In $B$ the sequence $1,2,3$ is a path from $n_1$ to $n_3$ of length $2$, and it is the only one.
In $H$ the sequence $v_1,v_3,v_4,v_5$ is a path \cite[p.~22]{petrosyan2015grafneri}.
\end{example}

\begin{nonexample}
In $B$ the sequence $1,2,3,2$ is a walk but not a path, because $n_2$ occurs twice.
In $H$ the trail $v_1,v_3,v_2,v_4,v_3,v_5$ is not a path, because $v_3$ occurs twice \cite[p.~22]{petrosyan2015grafneri}.
\end{nonexample}

\plainwords If you never pass a house twice, you never drive a road twice either.
The reason is short.
Driving a road twice means standing at both of its ends twice.
So every route is also a trip without repeated roads.

\begin{proposition}[a path is a trail]\label{prop:pathtrail}
In a simple graph, a walk whose vertices are pairwise distinct has pairwise distinct edges.
Hence every path is a trail, and every trail is a walk.
\end{proposition}

Grinberg states this as an exercise \cite{grinberg2025graph}; the proof is given here.

\begin{proof}
Let $u_0,\dots,u_k$ have distinct vertices, and take two steps $a<b$, that is, the edges $u_{a-1}u_a$ and $u_{b-1}u_b$.
The vertex $u_b$ has an index larger than $a$, so it differs from $u_a$ and from $u_{a-1}$, whose indices are at most $a$.
But $u_b$ is an end of the second edge and is not an end of the first.
So the two edges are different.
\end{proof}

\plainwords A cycle is a closed trip that never drives a road twice.
The textbook lets such a trip pass a house twice on the way.
A simple cycle is stricter.
It passes no house twice, except the start, which is also the end.
It is a ring of houses.

\begin{definition}[cycle and simple cycle; textbook Definitions~1.2.14 and 1.2.15]\label{def:cycle}
A closed trail is a \emph{cycle} (the textbook also says \emph{closed path}) \cite[p.~22]{petrosyan2015grafneri}.
A cycle is a \emph{simple cycle} if only its first and last vertices coincide \cite[p.~22]{petrosyan2015grafneri}; the textbook writes $C_n$ for the simple cycle on $n$ vertices, $n\ge 3$ \cite[p.~22]{petrosyan2015grafneri}.
\end{definition}

English usage differs: Grinberg's ``cycle'' is a closed walk of length at least $3$ with distinct vertices except the closing one, which is the textbook's simple cycle \cite{grinberg2025graph}.
This note follows the textbook and always writes ``simple cycle'' for the English ``cycle''.
Cycles are not needed for the main results; they are given because the textbook's build order contains them.

\begin{remark}
A cycle may repeat vertices (a figure eight is a cycle) and may not repeat edges.
The condition $n\ge 3$ excludes the degenerate cases: in a simple graph a closed trail cannot have length $1$ or $2$.
The textbook does not say whether a closed trail of length $0$ is a cycle; this note does not count it, as an interpretation.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}
In $T$ the sequence $1,2,3,1$ is a simple cycle of length $3$.
In $H$ the sequence $v_1,v_3,v_4,v_2,v_1$ is a simple cycle \cite[p.~22]{petrosyan2015grafneri}.
The sequence $v_3,v_2,v_1,v_3,v_5,v_4,v_3$ in $H$ is a cycle with six distinct edges that is not simple, because $v_3$ occurs three times \cite[p.~22]{petrosyan2015grafneri}.
\end{example}

\begin{nonexample}
In $B$ the closed walk $1,2,1$ is not a cycle, because the edge $n_1n_2$ is used twice.
In fact $B$ has no cycle: this was checked by script for all closed walks of length $1$ to $6$ (\cref{app:repro}).
\end{nonexample}

\subsection{Distance}\label{sec:distance}

\plainwords The distance between two houses is the number of roads on the shortest trip between them.
If there is no trip at all, we say the distance is infinite.
A house is at distance zero from itself.

\begin{definition}[distance; textbook Definition~1.2.16]\label{def:distance}
The \emph{distance} $d(u,v)$ between vertices $u$ and $v$ is the length of a shortest $(u,v)$-path if at least one exists, and $+\infty$ otherwise \cite[pp.~22--23]{petrosyan2015grafneri}.
\end{definition}

The textbook's ``path'' in this definition is its Definition~1.2.12 (trail).
The value does not depend on whether one reads trail or path, because the shortest walk, the shortest trail, and the shortest path between two vertices have the same length; this is proved in \cref{app:distance}.
The textbook lists three properties \cite[p.~23]{petrosyan2015grafneri}, proved from the definition in \cref{app:distance}:
\begin{enumerate}
\item $d(u,v)\ge 0$, and $d(u,v)=0$ if and only if $u=v$;
\item $d(u,v)=d(v,u)$;
\item $d(u,v)\le d(u,w)+d(w,v)$.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{remark}
The definition allows the value $+\infty$, which is how a missing route shows up.
It forbids negative values and any value that is not the length of an actual trip.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}
In $B$: $d(n_1,n_2)=1$, $d(n_2,n_3)=1$, $d(n_1,n_3)=2$, and $d(n_i,n_i)=0$.
The walk $1,2,1,2,3$ has length $4$, but the distance from $n_1$ to $n_3$ is $2$, the shortest.
\end{example}

\begin{nonexample}
The number $4$ is not the distance from $n_1$ to $n_3$ in $B$, although a walk of length $4$ exists: distance is the minimum, not any length.
In the graph $D$ with vertices $n_1,n_2,n_3$ and the single edge $n_1n_2$, the distance $d(n_1,n_3)$ is not a number but $+\infty$.
\end{nonexample}

\subsection{Every walk contains a path}\label{sec:walkpath}

\plainwords Suppose you can drive from one house to another.
Then you can do it without passing any house twice.
Look at your trip.
If it passes some house twice, you went round a loop.
Cut the loop out.
What is left is still a trip with the same start and the same end, and it is shorter.
Keep cutting until no loop is left.
The trip that remains is a route, and it is never longer than the first trip.

\begin{lemma}[textbook Lemma~1.2.1, with the length bound]\label{lem:walkpath}
Let $u$ and $v$ be vertices of a simple graph, equal or different.
If there is a $(u,v)$-walk of length $k$, then there is a $(u,v)$-path of length at most $k$, all of whose vertices and edges are vertices and edges of the walk.
\end{lemma}

The textbook states the lemma for $u\ne v$ and without a length bound, and says that from any $(u,v)$-walk one can extract a simple $(u,v)$-path \cite[p.~23]{petrosyan2015grafneri}.
Its proof is by induction on the length of the walk, cutting out the part between two equal vertices \cite[p.~23]{petrosyan2015grafneri}, and that proof yields the bound.
Grinberg's Corollary~2.9.7 states the version for all $u,v$ with the length bound \cite{grinberg2025graph}.
The case $u=v$ is needed here because a closed walk such as $2,1,2$ in $B$ has equal ends; the path it contains is the single vertex, of length $0$.

\begin{proof}
By strong induction on $k$ we prove the statement $S(k)$: every $(u,v)$-walk of length $k$, for any $u$ and $v$, contains a $(u,v)$-path of length at most $k$.

\emph{Base case $k=0$.}
A walk of length $0$ is a single vertex $u$, so $v=u$.
A single vertex has no repeated vertex, so the walk is itself a path, of length $0\le 0$.

\emph{Induction hypothesis.}
Let $k\ge 1$ and assume $S(m)$ for every $m<k$.

\emph{Step.}
Let $u_0,\dots,u_k$ be a $(u,v)$-walk, so $u_0=u$ and $u_k=v$.
If its vertices are pairwise distinct, it is a path of length $k$, and we are done.
Otherwise there are indices $i<j$ with $u_i=u_j$.
Form $W'=u_0,\dots,u_i,u_{j+1},\dots,u_k$; if $j=k$ nothing follows $u_i$ and $W'$ ends at $u_i$.
Then $W'$ is a $(u,v)$-walk: it starts at $u_0=u$; it ends at $v$, since its last vertex is $u_k=v$ when $j<k$ and is $u_i=u_j=u_k=v$ when $j=k$; and every two consecutive vertices are adjacent, since the only new consecutive pair is $(u_i,u_{j+1})=(u_j,u_{j+1})$, which is a step of the old walk.
The length of $W'$ is $i+(k-j)$, which is less than $k$ because $i<j$.
By the induction hypothesis $W'$ contains a $(u,v)$-path of length at most its own length, hence less than $k$.
Every vertex and edge of that path belongs to $W'$ and so to the old walk.
\end{proof}

Each cut removes at least one step, so the process stops after at most $k$ cuts, and a walk that cannot be cut has no repeated vertex.
As a trace, the textbook's walk $v_1,v_3,v_2,v_4,v_3,v_2,v_4,v_3,v_5$ of length $8$ has $u_1=u_4=v_3$; cutting gives $v_1,v_3,v_2,v_4,v_3,v_5$ of length $5$; cutting again gives $v_1,v_3,v_5$ of length $2$, a path.
In $B$, $1,2,1,2,3$ cuts to $1,2,3$, and the closed walk $2,1,2$ cuts to the single vertex $2$.

\plainwords A route visits each house at most once.
So a route cannot be longer than the number of houses minus one.
That tells us how far we ever need to look for a route.

\begin{corollary}[length of a path]\label{cor:bound}
In a graph with $N$ vertices every path has at most $N-1$ edges.
Hence if there is a walk from $u$ to $v$, there is a walk from $u$ to $v$ of length at most $N-1$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
A path of length $k$ lists $k+1$ pairwise distinct vertices, and there are only $N$ vertices, so $k+1\le N$.
For the second part apply \cref{lem:walkpath}: it gives a path inside the walk, of length at most that of the walk and, by the first part, at most $N-1$.
\end{proof}

Grinberg makes the same remark \cite{grinberg2025graph}.
For $B$, $N=3$, so a path has at most $2$ edges, and the longest path in $B$ has exactly $2$.

\subsection{Connected graphs and components}\label{sec:connecteddefs}

\plainwords Some villages are in one piece.
From any house you can reach any other house along roads.
Other villages are broken into pieces.
In a broken village some house cannot be reached from some other house.
Each piece, taken whole, is called a component.

\begin{definition}[connected graph; textbook Definition~2.1.1]\label{def:connected}
A graph $G=(V,E)$ is \emph{connected} if for any two vertices $u$ and $v$ of $G$ there is a $(u,v)$-path in $G$ \cite[p.~48]{petrosyan2015grafneri}.
\end{definition}

The board has the same definition: a graph is connected if for any $n_i,n_j$ there exists a path between the nodes \cite{whiteboard1,whiteboard2}.

\begin{remark}\label{rem:connected}
\begin{enumerate}
\item In the textbook the word ``path'' here is its Definition~1.2.12, a trail.
The course convention is no repeated vertex, and the board does not say which it uses.
The definition does not depend on the choice: a walk between two different vertices contains a path with distinct vertices (\cref{lem:walkpath}), and a walk with distinct vertices has distinct edges (\cref{prop:pathtrail}), so the two readings accept the same graphs.
\item ``Any two vertices'' includes $u=v$, and for $u=v$ the walk with one vertex and no edge is a $(u,u)$-path.
So these pairs are always satisfied, and reading the definition as ``any two different vertices'' changes nothing.
The textbook does not discuss $u=v$, so this reading is an interpretation.
\item The definition forbids exactly one thing: a pair of vertices with no trip between them.
\end{enumerate}
\end{remark}

\begin{example}
$B$ is connected.
The pair $n_1,n_3$ is joined by the path $1,2,3$, and the pairs $n_1,n_2$ and $n_2,n_3$ by one edge each.
The textbook's own examples are the complete graphs, the complete bipartite graphs, and three graphs of its Fig.~2.1.1 \cite[p.~48]{petrosyan2015grafneri}.
\end{example}

\begin{nonexample}
$D$, three vertices with only the edge $n_1n_2$, is not connected: no walk leaves $n_3$, so the pair $n_1,n_3$ has no path.
The textbook shows a graph that is not connected and has four components in its Fig.~2.1.2 \cite[p.~49]{petrosyan2015grafneri}.
\end{nonexample}

\plainwords Look at a broken village.
Say two houses belong together when one can reach the other.
Houses that belong together form a piece.
Nobody in one piece can reach anybody in another piece.

\begin{definition}[connected components; textbook, p.~49, in prose]\label{def:component}
On $V$ define $u\sim v$ to mean that $G$ has a $(u,v)$-path.
This relation is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive, so $V$ splits into classes $V_1,\dots,V_p$.
The subgraphs $G[V_j]$ with vertex set $V_j$ and all edges of $G$ that have both ends in $V_j$ are the \emph{connected components} of $G$ \cite[p.~49]{petrosyan2015grafneri}.
\end{definition}

The textbook gives this definition in a paragraph without a number.
The three properties of the relation hold because a single vertex is a path of length $0$, a path can be read backwards, and two walks that meet at a vertex join into one walk, which contains a path by \cref{lem:walkpath}.

\begin{remark}\label{rem:onecomponent}
A component cannot be enlarged: a vertex reachable from a vertex of $V_j$ lies in $V_j$.
The textbook's Remark~2.1.1 says that $G$ is connected if and only if it has exactly one component \cite[p.~49]{petrosyan2015grafneri}.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}
$D$ has the two components $D[\{n_1,n_2\}]$ and $D[\{n_3\}]$; $B$ has the single component $B[\{n_1,n_2,n_3\}]$.
\end{example}

\begin{nonexample}
$D[\{n_1\}]$ is not a component of $D$, although it is a connected subgraph: $n_2$ can be reached from $n_1$, so $n_2$ belongs to the same class.
A component is a whole class, not any connected piece.
\end{nonexample}
