\section{Question 1: what the entries of $A^k$ count}\label{sec:walks}

This section answers question 1.
The answer is one sentence: the entry $(A^k)_{ij}$ is the number of walks of length exactly $k$ from $n_i$ to $n_j$ (\cref{thm:walkcount}).
The rest of the section proves it, draws the consequences that \cref{sec:connectivity} needs, and shows that it counts walks and not paths.

\subsection{The walk-counting theorem}\label{sec:walkcounting}

\plainwords Take the table of roads and multiply it by itself.
Each number in the new table counts trips of two roads.
The trips run from the row house to the column house.
Multiply again, and you count trips of three roads.
The count includes trips that turn back.
So the table counts trips, not clean routes.

\begin{theorem}[walk counting]\label{thm:walkcount}
Let $A$ be the adjacency matrix of a simple undirected graph $G$ with vertices $n_1,\dots,n_N$, and let $k\ge 1$ be an integer.
Then $(A^k)_{ij}$ is the number of walks of length $k$ from $n_i$ to $n_j$, for all $i,j$.
\end{theorem}

Quinlan's course notes state and prove this theorem for simple graphs \cite[Theorem~1.1.3]{quinlan_matrices_graphs}; Grinberg states it for multidigraphs \cite[Theorem~4.5.10]{grinberg2025graph}, Lehman, Leighton, and Meyer for digraphs \cite[Corollary~9.3.3]{lehman_mcs_adjacency}, and Bai for simple graphs \cite[Theorem~1]{bai2025adjacency}.
The textbook does not state it \cite{petrosyan2015grafneri}, so it is proved here, along the route of Quinlan's proof.

\begin{remark}\label{rem:walkcount}
The theorem allows $i=j$, which counts closed walks, and it counts every walk once, with repeats of vertices and edges permitted.
It forbids reading the number as a count of paths (\cref{prop:warning}).
\end{remark}

\begin{example}
For $B$, $(A^2)_{13}=1$: the only walk of length $2$ from $n_1$ to $n_3$ is $1,2,3$.
\end{example}

\begin{nonexample}
$(A^2)_{12}=0$ in $B$ does not say that $n_1$ and $n_2$ are unconnected.
It says only that no walk of length exactly $2$ joins them; the walk $1,2$ of length $1$ and the walks $1,2,1,2$ and $1,2,3,2$ of length $3$ do exist.
\end{nonexample}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{thm:walkcount}, by induction on $k$]
Write $W_k(i,j)$ for the number of walks of length $k$ from $n_i$ to $n_j$.
We prove $W_k(i,j)=(A^k)_{ij}$ for all $i,j$, for each $k\ge 1$.
By \cref{rem:walk} we count vertex sequences $w_0,w_1,\dots,w_k$ with $w_0=n_i$, $w_k=n_j$, and $w_{t-1},w_t$ adjacent for each $t$.

\emph{Base case $k=1$.}
A walk of length $1$ from $n_i$ to $n_j$ is a sequence $(n_i,n_j)$ with $n_i,n_j$ adjacent.
There is exactly one such sequence if $n_i$ and $n_j$ are adjacent and none otherwise.
So $W_1(i,j)=A_{ij}=(A^1)_{ij}$.

\emph{Induction hypothesis.}
Let $k\ge 2$ and suppose $W_{k-1}(i,j)=(A^{k-1})_{ij}$ for all $i,j$.

\emph{Inductive step.}
Fix $i$ and $j$.
Every walk $w_0,\dots,w_k$ of length $k$ from $n_i$ to $n_j$ has a vertex $w_{k-1}$ just before the end.
Because $k\ge 2$, deleting the last vertex leaves a walk of length $k-1\ge 1$, to which the hypothesis applies.
Let $w_{k-1}=n_r$ with $r\in\{1,\dots,N\}$ and sort the walks into $N$ classes by the value of $r$.
The classes are disjoint, since each walk has exactly one second-last vertex, and together they contain every walk, so
\[
W_k(i,j)=\sum_{r=1}^{N}\bigl(\text{number of walks of length }k\text{ from }n_i\text{ to }n_j\text{ with second-last vertex }n_r\bigr).
\]
Fix $r$.
We claim that the number in class $r$ is $W_{k-1}(i,r)\,A_{rj}$.
\begin{itemize}
\item If $n_r$ and $n_j$ are not adjacent, so $A_{rj}=0$, no walk can have $n_r$ followed by $n_j$, so class $r$ is empty and its count is $0=W_{k-1}(i,r)\cdot 0$.
\item If $n_r$ and $n_j$ are adjacent, so $A_{rj}=1$, delete the last vertex of a walk in class $r$.
What remains is a sequence from $n_i$ to $n_r$ with consecutive vertices adjacent, that is, a walk of length $k-1$ from $n_i$ to $n_r$.
Conversely, a walk of length $k-1$ from $n_i$ to $n_r$ followed by $n_j$ has all consecutive pairs adjacent (the new pair $n_r,n_j$ is adjacent), so it is a walk of length $k$ from $n_i$ to $n_j$ with second-last vertex $n_r$.
Deleting and appending undo each other, so they match the two sets one to one, and the count is $W_{k-1}(i,r)=W_{k-1}(i,r)\cdot 1$.
\end{itemize}
Therefore $W_k(i,j)=\sum_{r}W_{k-1}(i,r)A_{rj}$.
By the induction hypothesis $W_{k-1}(i,r)=(A^{k-1})_{ir}$, so
\[
W_k(i,j)=\sum_{r=1}^{N}(A^{k-1})_{ir}A_{rj}=(A^{k-1}A)_{ij}=(A^k)_{ij}
\]
by \cref{def:product}.
This proves the statement for $k$, and by induction for all $k\ge 1$.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Why the argument is valid.}
The sum over the second-last vertex covers each walk exactly once, and the matching in the case $A_{rj}=1$ is a one-to-one correspondence; these two facts are the only places where the counting could go wrong.
Simplicity is used twice, as an interpretation of how the proof works: to identify a walk with its vertex sequence (\cref{rem:walk}), and so that $A_{rj}$, which counts the edges between $n_r$ and $n_j$, is $0$ or $1$.
Undirectedness is not used in the induction; it only makes $A$ symmetric, and the digraph versions of the theorem in the sources agree \cite{grinberg2025graph,lehman_mcs_adjacency}.

\subsection{Consequences}\label{sec:consequences}

\plainwords A count is never negative, and it is always a whole number.
The table of trips looks the same when flipped over its diagonal.
A positive number means that at least one such trip exists.

\begin{corollary}\label{cor:walkcount}
Let $k\ge 1$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Every entry of $A^k$ is a non-negative integer, and a sum of powers of $A$ has a positive $(i,j)$ entry if and only if at least one summand has.
\item $(A^k)_{ij}>0$ if and only if there is a walk of length exactly $k$ from $n_i$ to $n_j$.
\item $A^k$ is symmetric.
\end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Parts 1 and 2: by \cref{thm:walkcount} each entry is a count, and a sum of non-negative integers is positive exactly when some summand is.
Part 3: $(A^k)^{T}=(A^{T})^k=A^k$, because $A$ is symmetric (\cref{prop:rowsums}).
Alternatively, reversing a walk matches the walks from $n_i$ to $n_j$ with the walks from $n_j$ to $n_i$.
\end{proof}

The diagonal of $A^2$ has a simple meaning, stated in Quinlan's notes after the theorem \cite{quinlan_matrices_graphs}.
The walks of length $2$ from $n_i$ to itself are $n_i,n_r,n_i$ with $n_r$ a neighbour, one for each neighbour, so $(A^2)_{ii}=\deg(n_i)$.
Hence the sum of the diagonal entries of $A^2$ is the sum of the degrees, which is $2|E|$ (\cref{prop:rowsums}).
For $B$ the diagonal of $A^2$ is $(1,2,1)$, the degrees, and its sum is $4=2|E|$ (\cref{sec:example}).

\subsection{Walks are not paths}\label{sec:notpaths}

\plainwords A trip may turn back and pass the same house twice.
A route never passes a house twice.
The table counts trips, so it counts the turn-back trips too.
A house with one neighbour always has a two-road trip that goes out and returns.
That trip is not a route.

\begin{proposition}[the entry is not a count of paths or trails]\label{prop:warning}
In general $(A^k)_{ij}$ is not the number of paths, nor the number of trails, of length $k$ from $n_i$ to $n_j$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
We use $B$.
First, $(A^2)_{11}=1>0$, the walk $1,2,1$.
It repeats the vertex $n_1$, so it is not a path, and it repeats the edge $n_1n_2$, so it is not a trail.
No path and no trail of length $2$ from $n_1$ to $n_1$ exists, so the entry $1$ is not a count of either.
More generally, whenever $n_i$ has a neighbour, $(A^2)_{ii}=\deg(n_i)>0$ although a path of positive length cannot start and end at the same vertex.
Second, $(A^3)_{12}=2$, the walks $1,2,1,2$ and $1,2,3,2$; neither is a path or a trail, since each repeats a vertex and an edge.
\end{proof}

The number of walks from $n_1$ to $n_2$ grows with $k$ (the matrices satisfy $A^{k+2}=2A^k$ for $k=1,\dots,5$, by script), while the only path from $n_1$ to $n_2$ is the single edge, of length $1$.

What a positive entry does guarantee is that a walk of length $k$ exists, and then, by \cref{lem:walkpath}, that a path of length at most $k$ exists.
That is all that \cref{sec:connectivity} uses.
So the warning does not harm the connectivity test, but the value of an entry is not a path count.

\paragraph{A published statement that says paths.}
Paramadevan and Sotheeswaran state in their Theorem~1 that the entry of $A^l$ counts the ``different paths'' of length $l$ \cite{paramadevan2021adjacency}.
As written this is false for any meaning of ``path'' that forbids repeated vertices or repeated edges, by \cref{prop:warning}.
The correct reading is walks, and their own property 7 says walks \cite{paramadevan2021adjacency}.
This note does not rely on that Theorem~1.
\Cref{thm:walkcount} is a statement about a finite object and has also been checked by computer on all labelled simple graphs with $2,3,4,5$ vertices (\cref{app:checks}); this check supports the proof and does not replace it.
