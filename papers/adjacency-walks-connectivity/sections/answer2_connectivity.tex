\section{Question 2: deciding connectivity from $A$}\label{sec:connectivity}

This section answers question 2.
The answer is a test that uses only the matrix $A$: for $N\ge 2$, the graph is connected if and only if every off-diagonal entry of $A+A^2+\dots+A^{N-1}$ is positive (\cref{thm:test}).
The proof rests on two earlier results: walk counting (\cref{thm:walkcount}) and the lemma that every walk contains a path (\cref{lem:walkpath}).

\subsection{The running total}\label{sec:sums}

\plainwords We need a name for a running total.
It adds the counts of trips with one road, with two roads, and so on.
We stop one step short of the number of houses.
The whole check uses this one total.

\begin{definition}[the sum $S$]\label{def:S}
For a graph with $N\ge 2$ vertices let $S=A+A^2+\dots+A^{N-1}$, the sum of the first $N-1$ powers of $A$, added entry by entry.
This is notation introduced in this note and is not from the textbook.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:S}
By \cref{thm:walkcount}, $S_{ij}$ is the number of walks from $n_i$ to $n_j$ whose length is between $1$ and $N-1$.
Hence every entry of $S$ is a non-negative integer.
The diagonal of $S$ counts closed walks and plays no role in the test (\cref{rem:diagonal}).
\end{remark}

\begin{example}
For $B$ ($N=3$), $S=A+A^2=\MBS$, written out with the matrices in \cref{sec:example}.
\end{example}

\begin{nonexample}
For $N=3$ the matrix $A^2$ alone is not $S$.
In $B$, $A^2$ has the zero entry $(1,2)$ although $n_1$ and $n_2$ are joined by an edge, and $A$ alone has the zero entry $(1,3)$ although $n_1$ and $n_3$ are connected.
Either matrix alone would wrongly report ``not connected''.
\end{nonexample}

\subsection{The test and its proof}\label{sec:testproof}

\plainwords We want a quick check for a village.
We only have the table that says which houses share a road.
From the table we can count trips.
We count trips with one road, with two roads, and so on.
We add the counts for each pair of houses.
We never need trips longer than one road fewer than the number of houses.
A wasteful trip can always be shortened.
So longer trips tell us nothing new.

\begin{proposition}[one pair at a time]\label{prop:pair}
Let $G$ have $N\ge 2$ vertices and let $i\ne j$.
The following are equivalent.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] There is a path from $n_i$ to $n_j$ in $G$.
\item[(ii)] There is an integer $k$ with $1\le k\le N-1$ and $(A^k)_{ij}>0$.
\item[(iii)] $S_{ij}>0$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
\emph{(i)$\Rightarrow$(ii).}
A path, in either reading of the word (\cref{rem:connected}), is a walk, so there is a walk from $n_i$ to $n_j$.
By \cref{lem:walkpath} there is a path $P$ from $n_i$ to $n_j$ whose length $k$ is at most that walk's length, and by \cref{cor:bound} $k\le N-1$.
If $k$ were $0$, then $P$ would be a single vertex and $n_i=n_j$; so $k\ge 1$.
$P$ is a walk of length $k$, so $(A^k)_{ij}\ge 1$ by \cref{thm:walkcount}.

\emph{(ii)$\Rightarrow$(iii).}
$S_{ij}=A_{ij}+(A^2)_{ij}+\dots+(A^{N-1})_{ij}$ is a sum of non-negative integers (\cref{cor:walkcount}), so it is at least as large as the positive term from (ii).

\emph{(iii)$\Rightarrow$(ii).}
A sum of non-negative terms that is positive has a positive term $(A^k)_{ij}$ with $1\le k\le N-1$.

\emph{(ii)$\Rightarrow$(i).}
If $(A^k)_{ij}>0$ with $k\ge 1$, then by \cref{cor:walkcount} there is a walk of length $k$ from $n_i$ to $n_j$.
By \cref{lem:walkpath} it contains a path from $n_i$ to $n_j$ with distinct vertices.
A walk with distinct vertices has distinct edges (\cref{prop:pathtrail}), so it is a path in either reading of the word.
\end{proof}

\plainwords There is a second form of the check.
Let every house also stay where it is for one step.
Then count again.
It gives the same answer.

\begin{proposition}[the form with $I+A$]\label{prop:iplusa}
Let $G$ have $N\ge 2$ vertices, let $m\ge N-1$ be an integer, and let $i\ne j$.
Then $\bigl((I+A)^m\bigr)_{ij}>0$ if and only if $S_{ij}>0$.
Every diagonal entry of $(I+A)^m$ is at least $1$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Since $I$ commutes with $A$, expanding the product of $m$ factors $I+A$ gives, by choosing $I$ or $A$ from each factor, the sum of $\binom{m}{k}A^k$ over $k=0,\dots,m$, because the product with $k$ letters $A$ is $A^k$ and there are $\binom mk$ ways to choose which factors give $A$.
Thus
\[
(I+A)^m=\sum_{k=0}^{m}\binom{m}{k}A^k .
\]
For $i\ne j$ the identity contributes $0$ at position $(i,j)$, so $\bigl((I+A)^m\bigr)_{ij}=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\binom mk (A^k)_{ij}$.
Each coefficient $\binom mk$ with $1\le k\le m$ is a positive integer and each $(A^k)_{ij}$ is a non-negative integer, so the sum is positive if and only if some $(A^k)_{ij}$ with $1\le k\le m$ is positive.
If such a $k\le m$ exists, there is a walk, hence a path from $n_i$ to $n_j$ (\cref{prop:pair}), hence a positive $(A^{k'})_{ij}$ with $k'\le N-1$ (same proposition), so $S_{ij}>0$.
Conversely $S_{ij}>0$ gives a positive $(A^k)_{ij}$ with $k\le N-1\le m$.
On the diagonal the term $k=0$ contributes $\binom m0 I_{ii}=1$ and all other terms are non-negative.
\end{proof}

\plainwords Here is the check in one sentence.
Count trips of every length up to one fewer than the number of houses.
Add the counts for each pair of different houses.
If no pair has a total of zero, everyone can reach everyone.

\begin{theorem}[the connectivity test]\label{thm:test}
Let $G$ be a graph with $N\ge 2$ vertices and adjacency matrix $A$.
The following are equivalent.
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(a)] $G$ is connected.
\item[(b)] Every off-diagonal entry of $S=A+A^2+\dots+A^{N-1}$ is positive.
\item[(c)] Every entry of $(I+A)^{N-1}$ is positive.
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

Paramadevan and Sotheeswaran state (a)$\Leftrightarrow$(b) as their Theorem~2 \cite{paramadevan2021adjacency}, and MathWorld also states a matrix test for connectedness \cite{weisstein_connectedgraph}; Bai states (a)$\Leftrightarrow$(c) as his Theorem~2 \cite{bai2025adjacency}.
Their proofs say ``paths'' where walks are meant, and Paramadevan and Sotheeswaran pass from a non-zero entry to a path without the walk-to-path lemma, so these sources are cited for the statements only.
The proof printed here is this note's own and follows the premises of \cref{thm:walkcount} and \cref{lem:walkpath}.

\begin{remark}
The test allows any connected or disconnected simple undirected graph with at least two vertices.
It forbids using only one power, or stopping before $A^{N-1}$ (\cref{sec:sharp}).
\end{remark}

\begin{proof}
By \cref{def:connected} and \cref{rem:connected}, (a) says that for every pair of vertices there is a path between them, and for a pair with $u=v$ this is always true.
So (a) holds if and only if statement (i) of \cref{prop:pair} holds for every pair $i\ne j$.
By \cref{prop:pair} this is equivalent to (iii) for every $i\ne j$, which is (b).
By \cref{prop:iplusa} with $m=N-1$, (iii) for every $i\ne j$ is equivalent to positivity of every off-diagonal entry of $(I+A)^{N-1}$, and the diagonal entries are positive anyway; together this is (c).
\end{proof}

\plainwords Here is the check step by step.
First write the table of the village.
Then multiply the table by itself again and again.
After each step, add the new counts to the running total.
Stop after one step fewer than the number of houses.
Look at every pair of different houses.
If any total is zero, those two houses cannot reach each other.
If no total is zero, everyone can reach everyone.

\begin{definition}[matrix test for connectivity]\label{def:testprocedure}
\emph{Input:} the adjacency matrix $A$ of a graph with $N\ge 2$ vertices.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Set $P=A$ and $S=A$.
\item For $k=2,3,\dots,N-1$: replace $P$ by $PA$ (now $P=A^k$) and add $P$ to $S$.
\item Look at the entries $S_{ij}$ with $i\ne j$. If one of them is $0$, report ``not connected''. If none is $0$, report ``connected''.
\end{enumerate}
The equivalent form computes $(I+A)^{N-1}$ and reports ``connected'' if no entry is $0$.
\end{definition}

Correctness is \cref{thm:test}.
A zero at $(i,j)$ in step 3 says more than ``not connected'': by \cref{cor:components} below, $n_i$ and $n_j$ lie in different components.

\begin{remark}
The procedure allows any $N\ge 2$; for $N=2$ the loop in step 2 is empty and $S=A$.
It forbids reading the diagonal of $S$ (\cref{rem:diagonal}).
\end{remark}

\begin{example}
For $B$ and for $D$ the procedure is carried out with every matrix written out in \cref{ex:testB} and \cref{ex:testD}.
\end{example}

\begin{nonexample}
Applying only step 1, that is, looking at $A$ alone, is not the procedure: $B$ is connected, but $A_{13}=0$.
\end{nonexample}

\plainwords The check can say more than yes or no.
A zero in the total tells which two houses lie in different pieces.
A nonzero total tells that two houses lie in the same piece.

\begin{corollary}[components from $S$]\label{cor:components}
For any graph, connected or not, with $N\ge 2$ vertices and $i\ne j$: $S_{ij}>0$ if and only if $n_i$ and $n_j$ lie in the same component.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
\Cref{prop:pair} does not use connectivity of $G$: it says $S_{ij}>0$ if and only if there is a path from $n_i$ to $n_j$, and by \cref{def:component} this says that $n_i$ and $n_j$ are in the same class.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[distance from powers]\label{rem:distancepowers}
For $i\ne j$ in a connected graph, the smallest $k$ with $(A^k)_{ij}>0$ is the distance $d(n_i,n_j)$.
Two sources state this \cite{lehman_mcs_adjacency,paramadevan2021adjacency}.
Here it follows from \cref{cor:walkcount}, which says that this smallest $k$ is the length of a shortest walk, and from \cref{prop:shortest}, which says that the shortest walk has the length $d(n_i,n_j)$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[why the diagonal is skipped, and the cases $N=1$ and $N=2$]\label{rem:diagonal}
For $N=2$ and one edge, $S=A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ has zeros on the diagonal although the graph is connected, so the diagonal of $S$ cannot be required to be positive.
For $N\ge 3$ and connected $G$ the diagonal of $S$ is positive anyway, because every vertex has a neighbour and $(A^2)_{ii}=\deg(n_i)$, but the test does not need this.
For $N=1$ there are no pairs of different vertices and the graph is connected; $S$ is an empty sum and $(I+A)^0=I=(1)$ has no zero entry, so the second form of the test gives the right answer, but the first form is not defined by the formula.
Therefore the test is stated for $N\ge 2$.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Why $N-1$ is the right last power}\label{sec:sharp}

\plainwords Why stop at one road fewer than the number of houses?
Because a route cannot be longer than that.
Why not stop sooner?
Take four houses in a row.
To go from the first to the last, you must pass every house between.
That takes three roads.
If we counted only trips of up to two roads, we would miss this pair.
We would say the village is cut in two, and we would be wrong.

\Cref{cor:bound} and the proof of \cref{prop:pair} show that $N-1$ is enough: if some walk joins two different vertices, then a path of length at most $N-1$ does, so powers beyond $A^{N-1}$ cannot change which off-diagonal entries of $S$ are positive.
The next proposition shows that $N-1$ cannot be lowered.

\begin{proposition}[$N-1$ cannot be lowered to $N-2$]\label{prop:sharp}
For every $N\ge 3$ there is a connected graph with $N$ vertices for which $A+\dots+A^{N-2}$ has a zero off-diagonal entry.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Take the path graph with edges $n_1n_2,n_2n_3,\dots,n_{N-1}n_N$.
Each step of a walk changes the vertex number by exactly $1$.
To go from vertex $1$ to vertex $N$ the number must increase by $N-1$, so at least $N-1$ steps are needed.
A walk of length $k<N-1$ from $n_1$ to $n_N$ does not exist, so $(A^k)_{1N}=0$ for $1\le k\le N-2$ by \cref{thm:walkcount}.
Hence the $(1,N)$ entry of $A+\dots+A^{N-2}$ is $0$, although the graph is connected.
\end{proof}

\plainwords Here is the four-house village, with every table written out.
The first table counts trips of up to two roads.
It has a zero for the first and last house.
The second table counts trips of up to three roads.
It has no zero.

\begin{example}[the bound is needed: the path on four vertices]\label{ex:sharp}
Let $P_4$ have vertices $n_1,\dots,n_4$ and edges $n_1n_2,n_2n_3,n_3n_4$ ($N=4$).
Every product below was computed by script (\cref{app:repro}).
\[
A=\MPA,\quad A^2=\MPAtwo,\quad A^3=\MPAthree .
\]
\[
A+A^2=\MPSshort\ (N-2\text{ powers}),\qquad A+A^2+A^3=\MPSfull\ (N-1\text{ powers}).
\]
With $N-2=2$ powers the $(1,4)$ entry is $0$ and the test would wrongly say ``not connected''; with $N-1=3$ powers every entry is positive.
\end{example}

\subsection{Breadth-first search, the alternative}\label{sec:bfs}

\plainwords There is another way, and it needs no table of counts.
Stand at one house and mark it.
Then mark every house next to it.
Then mark every house next to those.
Keep going until no new house appears.
If every house is marked, the village is in one piece.

\begin{remark}[breadth-first search]\label{rem:bfs}
Breadth-first search (BFS) from a start vertex $s$ keeps a queue of discovered vertices, and repeatedly takes the vertex at the front and discovers its undiscovered neighbours.
The correctness statement in the lecture slides of Schost is that for every vertex $v$ there is a walk from $s$ to $v$ if and only if $v$ is discovered when BFS ends \cite{schost2026bfs}.
Hence $G$ is connected if and only if every vertex is discovered when BFS starts from any one vertex $s$ \cite{schost2026bfs}, and BFS also yields the components directly.
The matrix test and BFS agree on every labelled graph with at most $6$ vertices (\cref{sec:example}); this is a computer check and not a proof.
The cost of the two methods is compared in \cref{sec:cost}.
\end{remark}
